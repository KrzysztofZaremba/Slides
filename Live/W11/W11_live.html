Business Forecasting

Can a category improve the forecast?

Week 10 forecast the arrival delay at Mexico City airport, flights$arr_delay, from two numbers: the departure delay and the scheduled flight time.

flights$carrier adds who flies the plane. It has three categories:

flights$carrier Airlines in the category
“Domestic” the Mexican airlines: Aeroméxico, Volaris, Viva Aerobus, Magnicharters, Mas
“US” the US airlines: United, American, Delta, FedEx
“Other foreign” the other 20 foreign airlines: Avianca, Copa, Air Canada, LATAM, Iberia, Air France, Lufthansa…

The category is the airline’s country, not the route: an Aeroméxico flight from Los Angeles is “Domestic”.

A 0/1 regressor has two categories. flights$carrier has three; flights$weekday has seven. Can a regressor with several categories make the forecast of the arrival delay more accurate? A smaller miss means better plans for gates and ground crews.

From categories to dummies

Two categories, as in week 10: one 0/1 column, a dummy, is enough. flights$intl is 1 for a flight from abroad and 0 for a flight from Mexico, like dayoff for Ecobici.

flights$carrier has three categories, and one 0/1 column cannot tell three groups apart. So we need several dummies: one per category, 1 if the flight is in that category and 0 if not. lm() builds them automatically.

\[{\color{#e67e22}{\text{domestic}_i}} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if carrier}_i = \text{“Domestic”} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}\]

\[{\color{#1b7a3d}{\text{other}_i}} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if carrier}_i = \text{“Other foreign”} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}\]

\[{\color{#8e44ad}{\text{us}_i}} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if carrier}_i = \text{“US”} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}\]

Why one category must be left out

Each flight belongs to exactly one group, so the three dummies always add up to 1: \(\ \cssId{mt-dsum}{\text{domestic}_i + \text{other}_i + \text{us}_i} = \cssId{mt-one}{1}\).

  • dsum: One of the three is 1 and the other two are 0, for every flight.
  • one: The same value as the intercept’s column of 1s, row by row.

What each coefficient means

The category left out is the reference category. R takes the first in alphabetical order, “Domestic”, so the regression has dummies for the other two. \(\hat y\) is the predicted arr_delay:

\(\displaystyle \hat y_{\rlap{i}\phantom{\text{Other foreign}}} = b_0 + b_1 \times \text{other}_i + b_2 \times \text{us}_i\)

\(\displaystyle \hat y_{\rlap{\text{Domestic}}\phantom{\text{Other foreign}}} = b_0 + b_1 \times \overset{\hphantom{\text{other}_i}}{0} + b_2 \times \overset{\hphantom{\text{us}_i}}{0} = {\color{#2471a3}{b_0}}\)

A Domestic flight: every dummy is 0 Only the intercept is left. \(b_0\) is the mean delay of Domestic flights, the reference.

\(\displaystyle \hat y_{\text{Other foreign}} = b_0 + {\color{#1b7a3d}{b_1}} \times \overset{\hphantom{\text{other}_i}}{1} + b_2 \times \overset{\hphantom{\text{us}_i}}{0} = b_0 + {\color{#1b7a3d}{b_1}}\)

An Other foreign flight: other = 1 \(b_1 = \hat y_{\text{Other foreign}} - \hat y_{\text{Domestic}}\): the gap in mean delay between Other foreign and Domestic flights.

\(\displaystyle \hat y_{\rlap{\text{US}}\phantom{\text{Other foreign}}} = b_0 + b_1 \times \overset{\hphantom{\text{other}_i}}{0} + {\color{#8e44ad}{b_2}} \times \overset{\hphantom{\text{us}_i}}{1} = b_0 + {\color{#8e44ad}{b_2}}\)

A US flight: us = 1 \(b_2 = \hat y_{\text{US}} - \hat y_{\text{Domestic}}\): the gap in mean delay between US and Domestic flights.

The intercept is the mean of the reference category. Each coefficient is the gap between its category and the reference.

What a coefficient of 0 means

\[\hat y_i = b_0 + b_1\,\text{other}_i + b_2\,\text{us}_i\]

Each coefficient compares one group with the reference. A coefficient of 0 means no gap.

\(b_2 = 0: \quad \hat y_{\text{US}} = b_0 + b_1 \times 0 + {\color{#8e44ad}{0}} \times 1 = b_0 = \hat y_{\text{Domestic}}\)

US = Domestic A US flight gets the same prediction as a Domestic flight: on average they land equally late.

\(\hat y_{\text{US}} - \hat y_{\text{Other foreign}} = (b_0 + b_2) - (b_0 + b_1) = b_2 - b_1\)

Only against the reference \(b_2 = 0\) says nothing about US against Other foreign. That gap is \(b_2 - b_1\).

\(b_1 = b_2 = 0: \quad \hat y_{\text{Domestic}} = \hat y_{\text{Other foreign}} = \hat y_{\text{US}} = b_0\)

The category does not matter All three groups have the same mean, so the category does not help the forecast.

\(b_0 = 0: \quad \hat y_{\text{Domestic}} = 0\)

The intercept Domestic flights land on time on average. It says nothing about the gaps.

In data an estimate is never exactly 0: here \(\hat b_1 = 5.74\) and \(\hat b_2 = 7.22\). Next week: is an estimate far enough from 0 to say that the groups differ?

3 · Interactions: one slope per group

So far a category moved the line up or down. Now it can also change the slope.

Does one more kilometre cost the same time?

A delivery app promises each customer a delivery time. Its couriers ride bicycles or motorbikes. For each delivery the app records the distance and the minutes it took.

Does one more kilometre add the same number of minutes on a bicycle and on a motorbike? Would we expect the same effect?

No. A motorbike covers each kilometre faster, so each extra kilometre should add fewer minutes.

One slope: the same effect for both

The week 10 model, with a 0/1 regressor for motorbikes: \(\ \text{minutes}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{km}_i + \beta_2\,\text{moto}_i + u_i\)

  • One slope assumes one more kilometre adds the same \(\beta_1\) minutes on both: here 3.20 minutes.
  • The parallel lines miss both groups: too flat for the bicycles, too steep for the motorbikes.

Can we allow a different effect for each group? Yes: with an interaction.

Two slopes

The same deliveries, one line per group:

  • Each group gets its own slope: the bicycle line is steeper.
  • The two lines are not parallel, and they fit both groups.

How do we get two slopes in one regression?

One distance column, km, gives one slope for everyone. For two slopes we need two distance columns: km for everyone, and a second one that only applies to motorbikes. It equals the distance for a motorbike and 0 for a bicycle.

\[\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 5.9 & 0 & {\color{#e67e22}{0}} \\ 1 & 2.7 & 0 & {\color{#e67e22}{0}} \\ 1 & 1.1 & 0 & {\color{#e67e22}{0}} \\ 1 & 4.3 & 1 & {\color{#2471a3}{4.3}} \\ 1 & 5.3 & 1 & {\color{#2471a3}{5.3}} \\ 1 & 4.5 & 1 & {\color{#2471a3}{4.5}} \end{bmatrix} \begin{matrix} \text{bicycle} \\ \text{bicycle} \\ \text{bicycle} \\ \text{motorbike} \\ \text{motorbike} \\ \text{motorbike} \end{matrix}\]

Columns: the 1s for the intercept, km, moto, and the new column: the distance for a motorbike, 0 for a bicycle.

The new column is \(\text{km} \times \text{moto}\): the distance times the 0/1 regressor. The product of two regressors is called an interaction.

\[\text{minutes}_i = \cssId{mt-s0}{\beta_0} + \cssId{mt-s1}{\beta_1}\,\text{km}_i + \cssId{mt-s2}{\beta_2}\,\text{moto}_i + \cssId{mt-s3}{\beta_3}\,(\text{km}_i \times \text{moto}_i) + u_i\]

  • s0: The intercept: predicted minutes when every regressor is 0, a bicycle delivery of 0 km.
  • s1: Minutes per kilometre on a bicycle: for a bicycle, moto and the product are both 0.
  • s2: The extra minutes of a motorbike delivery at 0 km.
  • s3: The interaction: how many minutes per kilometre a motorbike differs from a bicycle.

In R, two ways give the same coefficients:

  • Build the column and add it: deliveries$km_moto <- deliveries$km * deliveries$moto, then lm(minutes ~ km + moto + km_moto, data = deliveries).
  • Or write the product in the formula: lm(minutes ~ km * moto, data = deliveries) adds km, moto and their product, which summary() calls km:moto.

What the regression gives for each group

\(\displaystyle \text{minutes}_{\rlap{i}\phantom{\text{motorbike}}} = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{km}_i + \beta_2 \times \text{moto}_i + \beta_3 \times \text{km}_i \times \text{moto}_i + u_i\)

\(\displaystyle \text{minutes}_{\rlap{\text{bicycle}}\phantom{\text{motorbike}}} = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{km}_i + \beta_2 \times \overset{\hphantom{\text{moto}_i}}{{\color{#e67e22}{0}}} + \beta_3 \times \text{km}_i \times \overset{\hphantom{\text{moto}_i}}{{\color{#e67e22}{0}}} + u_i\)

\(\displaystyle \phantom{\text{minutes}_{\text{motorbike}}} = \boxed{\beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{km}_i} + u_i\)

A bicycle: moto = 0 Both terms with moto drop out. The line starts at \(\beta_0\) and rises \(\beta_1\) minutes per kilometre.

\(\displaystyle \text{minutes}_{\rlap{\text{motorbike}}\phantom{\text{motorbike}}} = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{km}_i + \beta_2 \times \overset{\hphantom{\text{moto}_i}}{{\color{#2471a3}{1}}} + \beta_3 \times \text{km}_i \times \overset{\hphantom{\text{moto}_i}}{{\color{#2471a3}{1}}} + u_i\)

\(\displaystyle \phantom{\text{minutes}_{\text{motorbike}}} = \boxed{(\beta_0 + \beta_2) + (\beta_1 + \beta_3)\,\text{km}_i} + u_i\)

A motorbike: moto = 1 \(\beta_2\) moves the start of the line; \(\beta_3\) changes its slope.

\(\displaystyle \widehat{\text{minutes}}_{\rlap{\text{bicycle}}\phantom{\text{motorbike}}} = {\color{#e67e22}{6.44 + 4.84\,\text{km}_i}}\)

\(\displaystyle \widehat{\text{minutes}}_{\rlap{\text{motorbike}}\phantom{\text{motorbike}}} = {\color{#2471a3}{(6.44 + 1.50) + (4.84 - 3.31)\,\text{km}_i}}\)

The OLS estimates The motorbike line: \(7.94 + 1.53\,\text{km}\).

What \(\beta_2\) and \(\beta_3\) do to the lines

The 0/1 regressor works as a switch: anything multiplied by moto is 0 for bicycles and only counts for motorbikes.

\(\beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{km}\): one line, for bicycles

\(+\ \beta_2\,\text{moto}\): switched on for motorbikes, the line moves up

\(+\ \beta_3\,(\text{km} \times \text{moto})\): an extra slope, switched on for motorbikes, turns the line

Motorbikes: minutes per km 1.53 \(\hat\beta_1 + \hat\beta_3 = 4.84 - 3.31 = 1.53\)
  • The interaction gives exactly the two lines of the two separate regressions.
  • So \(\beta_2\) is the difference between the two starts, and \(\beta_3\) the difference between the two slopes, motorbike minus bicycle.
  • One regression instead of two: the difference in slopes now has its own coefficient, its own standard error and its own t-test.

Reading each coefficient

\[\widehat{\text{minutes}}_i = 6.44 + 4.84\,\text{km}_i + 1.50\,\text{moto}_i - 3.31\,(\text{km}_i \times \text{moto}_i)\]

  • \(\hat\beta_0 = 6.44\): a bicycle delivery of 0 km takes 6.4 minutes.
  • \(\hat\beta_1 = 4.84\): on a bicycle, each extra kilometre adds 4.84 minutes.
  • \(\hat\beta_2 = 1.50\): at 0 km, a motorbike delivery takes 1.50 minutes more than a bicycle one. It is the gap between the lines at 0 km only.
  • \(\hat\beta_3 = -3.31\): each extra kilometre adds 3.31 minutes less on a motorbike than on a bicycle, the reference group. It is a difference between two slopes, not the motorbike slope.

A negative \(\hat\beta_3\) does not mean that a motorbike delivery gets shorter with distance. The motorbike slope is \(\hat\beta_1 + \hat\beta_3 = 4.84 - 3.31 = 1.53\) minutes per kilometre: still positive, just smaller than on a bicycle.

The gap between the groups depends on the distance: \(\hat\beta_2 + \hat\beta_3 \times \text{km}\). At 5 km it is \(1.50 - 3.31 \times 5 = -15.06\) minutes: the motorbike is 15.1 minutes faster.

What a coefficient of 0 means in an interaction

\[\text{minutes}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{km}_i + \beta_2\,\text{moto}_i + \beta_3\,(\text{km}_i \times \text{moto}_i) + u_i\]

The lines if one coefficient were 0, the others at their estimates:

\({\color{#e67e22}{\text{bicycle: } \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{km}}}\)
\({\color{#2471a3}{\text{motorbike: } (\beta_0 + \beta_2) + \beta_1\,\text{km}}}\)

\(\beta_3 = 0\): the same slope for both, parallel lines. One more kilometre costs the same time on either vehicle.

\({\color{#e67e22}{\text{bicycle: } \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{km}}}\)
\({\color{#2471a3}{\text{motorbike: } \beta_0 + (\beta_1 + \beta_3)\,\text{km}}}\)

\(\beta_2 = 0\): the same start at 0 km. The lines open out from one point.

\({\color{#e67e22}{\text{bicycle: } \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{km}}}\)
\({\color{#2471a3}{\text{motorbike: } \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{km}}}\)

\(\beta_2 = \beta_3 = 0\): one line for both. The vehicle does not matter.

\(\beta_1 = 0\): distance does not matter for a bicycle, the reference group. The motorbike slope is then \(\beta_3\) alone. Whether \(\beta_3\) is 0 is the question of this section: the t-test on \(\hat\beta_3\) answers it.

Does local competition matter?

beer6 keeps one product from drinks, a six-pack of Dos Equis (six 355 ml bottles): 802 prices in 261 stores, November 2025 to February 2026.

  • beer6$rivals_2km counts the other stores of the PROFECO survey within 2 km.
  • beer6$type has three categories: “supermarket” (a supermarket chain, 238 stores), “convenience” (a convenience chain such as Oxxo or 7-Eleven, 12 stores) and “local” (a store outside the national chains, 11 stores). beer6$local is 1 for a local store.

Does one more store nearby change the price of the six-pack by the same number of pesos in all three types of store? Would we expect the same effect?

Probably not. A national chain, supermarket or convenience, sets its prices at head office, so the stores around one branch should barely matter. A local store sets its own price: alone, it can charge more; with rivals around, it must charge less.

Appendix — two slopes for soda and water

\[\text{price}_i = \cssId{mt-i0}{\beta_0} + \cssId{mt-i1}{\beta_1}\,\text{litres}_i + \cssId{mt-i2}{\beta_2}\,\text{water}_i + \cssId{mt-i3}{\beta_3}\,(\text{litres}_i \times \text{water}_i) + u_i\]

  • i0: The price of a soda with no litres. It only anchors the soda line.
  • i1: Pesos per litre for soda: for soda, water and the product are both 0.
  • i2: How far the water line starts below the soda line, at zero litres.
  • i3: The interaction: how many pesos per litre water differs from soda. Water’s slope is beta1 + beta3.
  • For soda (\(\text{water} = 0\)): \(\ \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{litres}\).
  • For water (\(\text{water} = 1\)): \(\ (\beta_0 + \beta_2) + (\beta_1 + \beta_3)\,\text{litres}\).
  • The product of the two regressors is one more column in \(\mathbf{X}\). Build it with arithmetic: sw$litres_water <- sw$litres * sw$water.

🖊️ Appendix exercise — Build the interaction

  3. Recover the soda slope and the water slope of the previous exercise from the coefficients.
  4. Is the difference between the two slopes significant at 5%?

Appendix — lines that are not parallel

  • A 0/1 regressor alone moves the line. Its product with litres turns the line.
  • The interaction answers “does the effect of size depend on the product?” Here, yes: soda charges more for each extra litre.
  • The same product works for two continuous regressors: the slope of one then changes with the level of the other.

Appendix — the 2026 tax on sugary drinks

  • On 1 January 2026 the tax (IEPS) on sugary drinks rose from 1.6451 to 3.0818 pesos per litre.
  • Sugar-free soft drinks, untaxed until then, now pay 1.5 pesos per litre. Water pays nothing.
  • VAT of 16% is charged on top of the tax. In a 600 ml bottle the extra tax is \(0.6 \times (3.0818 - 1.6451) \times 1.16 = 1.00\) peso.

Did stores pass the tax on to the shelf price? Comparing December with January is not enough: prices change in January for other reasons too.

Water pays no tax but goes through the same January. Its change shows what would have happened to soda without the tax.
