Week 11 — Multiple regression II: categories and interactions

Business Forecasting

Which airline lands latest?

Week 10 forecast the arrival delay at Mexico City airport from the departure delay and the scheduled time. flights$carrier puts each flight in one of five groups:

  • “Aeromexico”, “Volaris”, “Viva Aerobus”: the three Mexican airlines
  • “US airline”: United, American and Delta
  • “Other foreign”: every other foreign airline (Avianca, Copa, Air Canada, Iberia…)

An airline’s operations team wants to know where it stands: which airline lands latest on average, and which earliest?

One variable, five groups: how a regression reads it.

🖊️ Exercise 2 — The airline in a regression

lm() accepts a text column as a regressor. What do its coefficients mean?

  1. Regress flights$arr_delay on flights$carrier.
  2. Compare the intercept with the Aeromexico mean of Exercise 1.
  3. Compare the coefficient on carrierVolaris with the gap between the Volaris mean and the Aeromexico mean.
  4. Which airline has no coefficient of its own?
reg <- lm(arr_delay ~ carrier, data = flights)
summary(reg)

The intercept, -20.00, is the Aeromexico mean. Each coefficient is the gap between that airline’s mean and the Aeromexico mean: Volaris 12.48 = -7.51 − (-20.00). Aeromexico has no coefficient: it is the base.

One category is the base

\[\text{arr_delay}_i = \cssId{mt-c0}{\beta_0} + \cssId{mt-c1}{\beta_1}\,\text{other}_i + \cssId{mt-c2}{\beta_2}\,\text{us}_i + \cssId{mt-c3}{\beta_3}\,\text{viva}_i + \cssId{mt-c4}{\beta_4}\,\text{volaris}_i + \cssId{mt-cu}{u_i}\]

  • c0: The mean arrival delay of the base category, Aeromexico: all four dummies are 0.
  • c1: Other foreign airlines minus Aeromexico: the gap between the two means.
  • c2: US airlines minus Aeromexico.
  • c3: Viva Aerobus minus Aeromexico.
  • c4: Volaris minus Aeromexico.
  • cu: Everything else about the flight: its departure delay, its route, the weather.
carrier Mean arrival delay Coefficient
Aeromexico -20.00 base
Other foreign -9.62 10.38
US airline -8.08 11.91
Viva Aerobus -3.08 16.92
Volaris -7.51 12.48
  • Five categories need four 0/1 regressors. The one left out is the base; every coefficient is a gap to it.
  • R leaves out the first category in alphabetical order: here, Aeromexico.

Why one category must be left out

Each flight belongs to exactly one airline, so the five dummies always add up to 1:

\[\cssId{mt-dsum}{\text{aeromexico}_i + \text{other}_i + \text{us}_i + \text{viva}_i + \text{volaris}_i} = \cssId{mt-one}{1}\]

  • dsum: One of the five is 1 and the other four are 0, for every flight.
  • one: The same value as the intercept’s column of 1s, row by row.

The column of 1s for the intercept is already in \(\mathbf{X}\). What happens if all five dummies go in with it?

Are the gaps fair?

Aeromexico’s flights land earliest. But the airlines may differ in more than the airline: how late their flights leave, and how long they fly.

Which airline lands latest for the same departure delay and the same scheduled time?

Raw gap and fair gap

  • For the same departure delay and scheduled time, Volaris lands 11.1 minutes later than Aeromexico and Viva Aerobus 10.2: Aeromexico builds more slack into its schedules, or makes up more time.
  • The rule of week 10 holds for each dummy: a gap moves when a left-out cause differs across the groups.
  • Volaris against Viva Aerobus? \(11.10 - 10.19 = 0.90\) minutes. Whether that gap is significant needs week 12.

2 · Categories and fit

A category with many groups uses many degrees of freedom. Does it earn them?

Does the day of the week matter?

flights$weekday has seven categories; R takes Friday, first in alphabetical order, as the base. That is six dummies.

Which earns its place in the forecast of week 10: the day of the week or the airline?

🖊️ Exercise 5 — Days, airlines and adjusted R²

  1. Regress flights$arr_delay on flights$dep_delay and flights$sched_time. Note the adjusted \(R^2\).
  2. Add flights$weekday. Note the adjusted \(R^2\). Which days differ from Friday at 5%?
  3. Replace weekday with carrier. Note the adjusted \(R^2\).
reg <- lm(arr_delay ~ dep_delay + sched_time, data = flights)
summary(reg)

reg_day <- lm(arr_delay ~ dep_delay + sched_time + weekday, data = flights)
summary(reg_day)

reg_air <- lm(arr_delay ~ dep_delay + sched_time + carrier, data = flights)
summary(reg_air)

dep_delay + sched_time 0 0.7687

The days add almost nothing: only Thursday (1.54 minutes, \(T = 3.57\)) and Saturday (0.95, \(T = 2.03\)) differ from Friday at 5%. The airline, with fewer dummies, raises the adjusted \(R^2\) by 0.032.

Each group costs a degree of freedom

  • A category with \(g\) groups adds \(g - 1\) regressors. Adjusted \(R^2\) charges for each one.
  • The airline is worth its four: the forecast’s typical miss falls from 13.4 to 12.5 minutes.
  • The day of the week is not: six dummies for a typical miss of 13.4 minutes.

Do the six days matter together, even if most are not significant one by one? That is one test of several coefficients at once: week 12.

When the effect of one regressor depends on another.

Is a litre worth the same in soda and in water?

sw holds the soft drinks and the bottled water of November 2025, one bottle at a time, up to 3 litres: 7,585 prices.

In week 10, a 0/1 regressor moved the whole line and kept its slope. Does an extra litre add the same number of pesos to a soda and to a bottle of water?

🖊️ Exercise 6 — One slope or two?

  1. Regress price on litres for the soda only, and then for the water only.
  2. Create sw$water, 1 for water and 0 for soda. Regress sw$price on sw$litres and sw$water.
  3. How many pesos per litre does each model give for soda? For water?

sw[sw$product == "soda", ] keeps the soda. (sw$product == "water") * 1 makes the 0/1 column.

reg_soda <- lm(price ~ litres, data = sw[sw$product == "soda", ])
summary(reg_soda)
reg_water <- lm(price ~ litres, data = sw[sw$product == "water", ])
summary(reg_water)

sw$water <- (sw$product == "water") * 1
reg <- lm(price ~ litres + water, data = sw)
summary(reg)

Alone, soda adds 8.29 pesos per litre and water 5.95. The model with a 0/1 regressor forces one slope on both: 8.20 pesos per litre, close to the soda slope because most rows are soda.

Two slopes in one regression

\[\text{price}_i = \cssId{mt-i0}{\beta_0} + \cssId{mt-i1}{\beta_1}\,\text{litres}_i + \cssId{mt-i2}{\beta_2}\,\text{water}_i + \cssId{mt-i3}{\beta_3}\,(\text{litres}_i \times \text{water}_i) + u_i\]

  • i0: The price of a soda with no litres. It only anchors the soda line.
  • i1: Pesos per litre for soda: for soda, water and the product are both 0.
  • i2: How far the water line starts below the soda line, at zero litres.
  • i3: The interaction: how many pesos per litre water differs from soda. Water’s slope is beta1 + beta3.
  • For soda (\(\text{water} = 0\)): \(\ \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{litres}\).
  • For water (\(\text{water} = 1\)): \(\ (\beta_0 + \beta_2) + (\beta_1 + \beta_3)\,\text{litres}\).
  • The product of the two regressors is one more column in \(\mathbf{X}\). Build it with arithmetic: sw$litres_water <- sw$litres * sw$water.

🖊️ Exercise 7 — Build the interaction

Does an extra litre add fewer pesos to water than to soda? By how many?

  1. Create sw$water and sw$litres_water, the product of sw$litres and sw$water.
  2. Regress sw$price on sw$litres, sw$water and sw$litres_water.
  3. Recover the soda slope and the water slope of Exercise 6 from the coefficients.
  4. Is the difference between the two slopes significant at 5%?
sw$water <- (sw$product == "water") * 1
sw$litres_water <- sw$litres * sw$water

reg <- lm(price ~ litres + water + litres_water, data = sw)
summary(reg)

8.2923 + (-2.3401)

Soda: 8.29 pesos per litre. Water: 8.29 + (-2.34) = 5.95, the slopes of the two separate regressions. The interaction has \(T = -8.93\): an extra litre of water adds 2.34 pesos less than an extra litre of soda.

Lines that are not parallel

  • A 0/1 regressor alone moves the line. Its product with litres turns the line.
  • The interaction answers “does the effect of size depend on the product?” Here, yes: soda charges more for each extra litre.
  • The same product works for two continuous regressors: the slope of one then changes with the level of the other.

Do local stores price to their neighbourhood?

Week 10 found that the national chains set national prices: municipal income barely moved the price of a soda.

wat holds the bottled water up to 1.5 litres, November 2025 to February 2026: 8,522 prices, 677 of them in local stores.

Does the price rise with municipal income in local stores, when it does not in the chains?

🖊️ Exercise 8 — Local stores and income

  1. Regress wat$price on wat$litres and wat$income.
  2. Create wat$income_local, the product of wat$income and wat$local. Regress wat$price on wat$litres, wat$income, wat$local and wat$income_local.
  3. How many pesos does 1,000 pesos more of municipal income add in a chain store? In a local store?
  4. Above what income is a local store dearer than a chain store, for the same bottle?

A local store is dearer when the coefficient on local, plus the coefficient on income_local times the income, is above zero.

reg1 <- lm(price ~ litres + income, data = wat)
summary(reg1)

wat$income_local <- wat$income * wat$local
reg2 <- lm(price ~ litres + income + local + income_local, data = wat)
summary(reg2)

2.6809 / 0.3386

Alone, income moves the price by -0.036 pesos per 1,000 pesos. In a chain store: -0.046. In a local store: -0.046 + 0.339 = 0.293 pesos per 1,000 pesos of income. The interaction is significant (\(T = 5.55\)). Local stores are cheaper in poorer municipalities and dearer above about 7.9 thousand pesos: 2.68 / 0.339.

Chains set one price; local stores follow the neighbourhood

  • An interaction of a 0/1 regressor with a continuous one gives each group its own slope.
  • For a bottler: in local stores the shelf price follows local income; in the chains it does not.

4 · Two groups, two periods

An interaction of two 0/1 regressors, and what a new tax did to prices.

The 2026 tax on sugary drinks

  • On 1 January 2026 the tax (IEPS) on sugary drinks rose from 1.6451 to 3.0818 pesos per litre.
  • Sugar-free soft drinks, untaxed until then, now pay 1.5 pesos per litre. Water pays nothing.
  • VAT of 16% is charged on top of the tax. In a 600 ml bottle the extra tax is \(0.6 \times (3.0818 - 1.6451) \times 1.16 = 1.00\) peso.

Did stores pass the tax on to the shelf price? Comparing December with January is not enough: prices change in January for other reasons too.

Water pays no tax but goes through the same January. Its change shows what would have happened to soda without the tax.

🖊️ Exercise 9 — Did the price of a soda jump?

tax600 holds the 600 ml bottles of soda and water in December 2025 and January 2026. tax600$group is “sugary soda”, “sugar-free soda” or “water”; tax600$after is 1 in January.

  1. Compute the mean price of sugary soda and of water, in December and in January.
  2. Keep the sugary soda and the water: sw600 <- tax600[tax600$group != "sugar-free soda", ]. Create sw600$sugar (1 for sugary soda) and sw600$sugar_after, the product of sugar and after.
  3. Regress sw600$price on sugar, after and sugar_after. Match each coefficient to the four means.
  4. Compare the coefficient on sugar_after with the extra tax in the bottle, 1.00 peso.
mean(tax600$price[tax600$group == "sugary soda" & tax600$after == 0])
mean(tax600$price[tax600$group == "sugary soda" & tax600$after == 1])

Water rose from 10.63 to 10.93 pesos (\(\beta_2 = 0.29\)); sugary soda from 19.32 to 21.04. The extra rise of soda is \(\beta_3 = 1.42\) pesos (\(T = 10.8\)), more than the 1.00 peso of extra tax: the stores passed on more than the tax.

  • \(\beta_3\) is a difference in differences: (soda’s change) − (water’s change).

🖊️ Exercise 10 — Sugar-free soda

Sugar-free soda went from no tax to 1.5 pesos per litre: 1.04 peso in a 600 ml bottle, with VAT. Did the stores pass that on?

  1. Keep the sugar-free soda and the water: dw600 <- tax600[tax600$group != "sugary soda", ].
  2. Build the 0/1 column for sugar-free soda and its product with after.
  3. Regress the price on the three regressors and read the extra change for sugar-free soda.
  4. Compare it with 1.04 peso, and with the sugary soda of Exercise 9.
dw600 <- tax600[tax600$group != "sugary soda", ]
dw600$sfree <- (dw600$group == "sugar-free soda") * 1
dw600$sfree_after <- dw600$sfree * dw600$after

reg <- lm(price ~ sfree + after + sfree_after, data = dw600)
summary(reg)

The extra change for sugar-free soda is 0.75 peso (\(T = 3.9\)), less than the 1.04 peso of new tax. Sugary soda rose by more than its tax; sugar-free soda by less than its tax.

What the tax did

  • The dashed line is the comparison: soda moving as water did. The gap at January is \(\beta_3 = 1.42\) pesos.
  • For a bottler or a retailer: the 2026 tax reached the shelf in full, and more, on sugary drinks.
  • It only works if soda and water would have moved together without the tax. Water is the stand-in for what soda would have done.

What you should be able to do

  • Put a regressor with several categories into lm() and read each coefficient as a gap to the base category.
  • Say why one category must be left out.
  • Explain why a gap between groups changes when other regressors are held fixed.
  • Use adjusted \(R^2\) to judge whether a category earns its dummies.
  • Build an interaction with arithmetic and read each group’s slope.
  • Read an interaction of two 0/1 regressors as a difference in differences, from the four means or from the regression.

[Spring 2026 final, Q13–Q18]

A Mexican manufacturer exports cars and boats to the United States. In the second quarter of 2025 the US put a tariff on cars, not on boats. exports holds the quarterly sales of each product, 2002 to 2026 (simulated exam data): exports$Sales, exports$car (1 for cars) and exports$after_q2_2025 (1 from 2025 Q2).

  1. Match each combination of (car, after_q2_2025) — (0,0), (1,0), (0,1), (1,1) — to boats or cars, before or after the tariff.

  2. Build the product of car and after_q2_2025 and regress Sales on the three regressors. What are the predicted boat sales before the tariff? After?

  3. Interpret the coefficient on after_q2_2025.

  4. What are the predicted car sales before the tariff?

  5. Interpret the coefficient on car.

  6. What is the effect of the tariff on car sales?

exports$car_after <- exports$car * exports$after_q2_2025
reg <- lm(Sales ~ car + after_q2_2025 + car_after, data = exports)
summary(reg)

new_data <- data.frame(car = c(0, 0, 1, 1), after_q2_2025 = c(0, 1, 0, 1),

predict(reg, new_data)

  2. Boats before: \(\beta_0 = 61{,}548\). After: \(61{,}548 + 17{,}097 = 78{,}645\).

  4. \(61{,}548 + 30{,}304 = 91{,}852\).

  5. Before the tariff, cars sold 30,304 more per quarter than boats.

  6. The coefficient on the product: cars changed by 40,037 less than boats, so the tariff cut car sales by about 40,000 per quarter (\(T = -3.35\)).
