Since W07, one regressor has forecast the daily trips:

\[\widehat{\text{trips}} = 16{,}893 + 723.6 \times \text{temp}, \qquad R^2 = 0.088\]

Temperature explains 8.8% of the day-to-day movement in trips. The fleet manager needs a better forecast.

What else moves the number of trips on a given day? Write down your guesses. For each one: can we measure it, and which way should it push trips?

From guesses to a regression

Take two guesses: days off and pollution.

  • The outcome \(y\): trips that day.
  • The regressors: temperature, a day off (0 or 1), pollution that day (PM2.5, in μg/m³).

The regression to write:

\[\text{trips}_i = \beta_0 + \cssId{mt-g1}{\beta_1}\,\text{temp}_i + \cssId{mt-g2}{\beta_2}\,\text{dayoff}_i + \cssId{mt-g3}{\beta_3}\,\text{pm25}_i + u_i\]

Each \(\beta\) is the change in trips when only its own variable changes and the others are held fixed. OLS finds all four numbers at once.

Build it by ticking boxes

  • g1: Trips for one more degree, comparing days of the same kind (working day or day off) with the same pollution.
  • g2: The gap between a day off and a working day at the same temperature and the same pollution.
  • g3: Trips for one more μg/m³ of PM2.5, at the same temperature, on the same kind of day.

How late will this flight land?

You plan the day for an airline at Mexico City airport (AICM). A flight from Monterrey left 40 minutes late. The gate, the ground crew and the passengers with a connection all need one number: how many minutes late will it land?

What moves the arrival delay? Write down your guesses. For each one: can we measure it, and which way should it push the delay?

Each cause needs its own number, measured with the others held fixed.

The data: arrivals at AICM

flights holds 11,887 flights that landed at Mexico City airport (AICM) in October 2025, from 111 airports. One row is one flight.

Column What it is
arr_delay minutes late at arrival (negative = early)
dep_delay minutes late at departure (negative = early)
sched_time scheduled flight time, minutes
origin, code, country, intl where the flight comes from; intl is 1 for a flight from outside Mexico
airline the airline
date, weekday, hour day and hour of the scheduled arrival
rain, wind, temp weather at AICM in that hour: rain (mm), wind (km/h), temperature (°C)

Schedules include slack, so most flights land before the scheduled time: only 14% land late.

Sources: arrival records of a public flight tracker; weather from the Open-Meteo historical archive. 359 records with extreme or impossible times are left out.

1 · How OLS finds the coefficients

Before the flights: how OLS finds several coefficients at once. The same rule as W07, the smallest sum of squared residuals.

Which three numbers?

A forecast for each flight needs three numbers:

\[\widehat{\text{arr_delay}}_i = b_0 + b_1\,\text{dep_delay}_i + b_2\,\text{sched_time}_i\]

Any three numbers give a forecast for each of the 11,887 flights, and an error for each: \(e_i = \text{arr_delay}_i - \widehat{\text{arr_delay}}_i\).

Guess b₀ b₁ b₂ Monterrey flight SSE, millions Typical miss
every flight lands on time 0 0 0 0 11.65 31.3
every flight lands at the average delay −14.2 0 0 −14.2 9.25 27.9
30 minutes early, plus 0.8 of the departure delay −30 0.8 0 2 2.39 14.2

\(SSE = \sum e_i^2\), the sum of the squared errors. Typical miss \(= \sqrt{SSE/n}\), in minutes. The Monterrey flight left 40 minutes late and is scheduled for 100 minutes.

The best three numbers bring the \(SSE\) down to 2.14 million, a typical miss of 13.4 minutes. Which three are they?

Still least squares

\[SSE(b_0, b_1, b_2) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( \text{arr_delay}_i - b_0 - b_1\,\text{dep_delay}_i - b_2\,\text{sched_time}_i \right)^2\]

Write \(y_i\) for \(\text{arr_delay}_i\), \(x_{i1}\) for \(\text{dep_delay}_i\) and \(x_{i2}\) for \(\text{sched_time}_i\):

\[SSE(b_0, b_1, b_2) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_{i1} - b_2 x_{i2} \right)^2\]

  • OLS picks the three numbers that make \(SSE\) as small as possible.
  • With one regressor this gave the W07 formulas for \(\hat{\beta}_0\) and \(\hat{\beta}_1\).
  • With two, it can still be solved by hand. With more, the answer is easiest to write with matrices.

Solve it by hand, without matrices

The flights as matrices

Stack the 11,887 flights, one row per flight. Hover over an entry to see what it is.

\[\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} \cssId{mt-mf-y1}{y_1} \\ \cssId{mt-mf-y2}{y_2} \\ \vdots \\ \cssId{mt-mf-yn}{y_n} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -15 \\ -14 \\ \vdots \\ 99 \end{bmatrix} \qquad \mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} \cssId{mt-mf-one}{1} & \cssId{mt-mf-x11}{x_{11}} & \cssId{mt-mf-x12}{x_{12}} \\ 1 & \cssId{mt-mf-x21}{x_{21}} & \cssId{mt-mf-x22}{x_{22}} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & \cssId{mt-mf-xn1}{x_{n1}} & \cssId{mt-mf-xn2}{x_{n2}} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 6 & 99 \\ 1 & 10 & 221 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & 132 & 94 \end{bmatrix}\]

  • \(\mathbf{y}\) is \(n \times 1\): the outcome, arr_delay, one entry per flight.
  • \(\mathbf{X}\) is \(n \times 3\): a column of 1s for the intercept, then one column per regressor, dep_delay and sched_time.
  • \(x_{ij}\) is regressor \(j\) of flight \(i\): the row is the flight, the column is the regressor. \(x_{21} = 10\): flight 2 left 10 minutes late.
  • mf-y1: y₁, the outcome of observation 1. Flight 1 came from Tuxtla Gutierrez: its arrival delay is -15 minutes (negative = early).
  • mf-y2: y₂, the outcome of observation 2. Flight 2 came from Panama City: its arrival delay is -14 minutes.
  • mf-yn: yₙ, the outcome of the last observation, flight 11,887, from Villahermosa: 99 minutes late.
  • mf-one: A 1 in every row: the column of the intercept. It multiplies β₀, so every flight gets the same β₀.
  • mf-x11: x₁₁, regressor 1 (dep_delay) of observation 1: flight 1 left 6 minutes late.
  • mf-x12: x₁₂, regressor 2 (sched_time) of observation 1: flight 1 is scheduled for 99 minutes.
  • mf-x21: x₂₁, regressor 1 (dep_delay) of observation 2: flight 2 left 10 minutes late.
  • mf-x22: x₂₂, regressor 2 (sched_time) of observation 2: flight 2 is scheduled for 221 minutes.
  • mf-xn1: xₙ₁, regressor 1 (dep_delay) of the last flight: it left 132 minutes late.
  • mf-xn2: xₙ₂, regressor 2 (sched_time) of the last flight: it is scheduled for 94 minutes.

The regression in matrix form

\[\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ \vdots \\ y_n \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{y}:\ n \times 1} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_{11} & x_{12} & \cdots & \cssId{mt-mf-x1k}{x_{1k}} \\ 1 & x_{21} & x_{22} & \cdots & x_{2k} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ 1 & x_{n1} & x_{n2} & \cdots & x_{nk} \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{X}:\ n \times (k+1)} \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \cssId{mt-mf-b0}{\beta_0} \\ \cssId{mt-mf-b1}{\beta_1} \\ \cssId{mt-mf-b2}{\beta_2} \\ \vdots \\ \cssId{mt-mf-bk}{\beta_k} \end{bmatrix}}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}:\ (k+1) \times 1} + \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \cssId{mt-mf-u1}{u_1} \\ u_2 \\ \vdots \\ u_n \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{u}:\ n \times 1}\]

  • One equation for all 11,887 flights at once.
  • With \(k\) regressors, \(\mathbf{X}\) has a column of 1s and then one column per regressor: \(k + 1\) columns. \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) has \(k + 1\) entries.
  • The flights have \(k = 2\): \(x_{i1}\) is dep_delay and \(x_{i2}\) is sched_time.

What \(\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\) gives, row by row

  • mf-x1k: x₁ₖ, regressor k of observation 1. The flights have k = 2, so this is x₁₂, the scheduled time of flight 1.
  • mf-b0: β₀, the intercept: the same for every flight.
  • mf-b1: β₁, the slope of regressor 1 with the others held fixed. For the flights: minutes of arrival delay per minute of departure delay.
  • mf-b2: β₂, the slope of regressor 2 with the others held fixed. For the flights: minutes of arrival delay per scheduled minute.
  • mf-bk: βₖ, the slope of the last regressor, k. The flights have k = 2, so βₖ is β₂.
  • mf-u1: u₁: everything else that moves the arrival of flight 1: the route, the airline, the weather, luck.

The errors, as a function of \(\mathbf{b}\)

Pick any guess \(\mathbf{b} = (b_0, b_1, \dots, b_k)\). Every flight gets a forecast and an error.

\(\displaystyle \hat{\mathbf{y}} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} b_0 + b_1 x_{11} + \cdots + b_k x_{1k} \\ \vdots \\ b_0 + b_1 x_{n1} + \cdots + b_k x_{nk} \end{bmatrix}\)

The forecasts Row \(i\) of \(\mathbf{X}\) times \(\mathbf{b}\): the forecast for flight \(i\).

\(\displaystyle \mathbf{e}(\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} y_1 - b_0 - b_1 x_{11} - \cdots - b_k x_{1k} \\ \vdots \\ y_n - b_0 - b_1 x_{n1} - \cdots - b_k x_{nk} \end{bmatrix}\)

The errors Each flight’s outcome minus its forecast. Change \(\mathbf{b}\) and every error changes.

\(\displaystyle SSE(\mathbf{b}) = \sum_i e_i^2 = \begin{bmatrix} e_1 & \cdots & e_n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} e_1 \\ \vdots \\ e_n \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{e}^\top \mathbf{e}\)

The sum of squares A row times a column: \(e_1 e_1 + \cdots + e_n e_n\).

\(\displaystyle \boxed{SSE(\mathbf{b}) = (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{b})^\top (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{b})}\)

A function of \(\mathbf{b}\) only \(\mathbf{y}\) and \(\mathbf{X}\) are the data and stay fixed. OLS picks the \(\mathbf{b}\) that makes this smallest.

Take the derivative

At the smallest \(SSE\), the derivative with respect to \(\mathbf{b}\) is zero, as in W07. Here it is in matrices.

\(\displaystyle SSE(\mathbf{b}) = (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{b})^\top (\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{b})\)

Start from the errors The sum of squared errors, as a function of \(\mathbf{b}\).

\(\displaystyle = \mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} + \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{b}\)

Multiply out Like \((a - c)(a - c)\), with \((\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b})^\top = \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\).

\(\displaystyle = \mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{y} - 2\,\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} + \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{b}\)

The two middle terms are equal \(\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{X}\mathbf{b}\) is one number, so it equals its own transpose, \(\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\).

\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial SSE}{\partial \mathbf{b}} = -2\,{\color{#2471a3}{\mathbf{X}^\top}}{\color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{y}}} + 2\,{\color{#2471a3}{\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}}}\,\mathbf{b}\)

Derivative in \(\mathbf{b}\) One derivative per coefficient, stacked in a column. As with one number: \(-2bc\) gives \(-2c\), and \(ab^2\) gives \(2ab\).

\(\displaystyle -2\,{\color{#2471a3}{\mathbf{X}^\top}}({\color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{y}}} - {\color{#2471a3}{\mathbf{X}}}\mathbf{b}) = \mathbf{0} \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{{\color{#2471a3}{\mathbf{X}^\top}} \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}}\)

Set it to zero, factor out \(-2\mathbf{X}^\top\) \(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{b}\) is \(\mathbf{e}\). At the OLS coefficients, \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}\).

The condition behind OLS

Write \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}\) out. Row \(j\) of \(\mathbf{X}^\top\) is column \(j\) of \(\mathbf{X}\): one regressor, across all flights.

\[\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{e} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1 \\ x_{11} & x_{21} & \cdots & x_{n1} \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\ x_{1k} & x_{2k} & \cdots & x_{nk} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} e_1 \\ e_2 \\ \vdots \\ e_n \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cssId{mt-xe-sum0}{\sum_i e_i} \\ \cssId{mt-xe-sum1}{\sum_i x_{i1} e_i} \\ \vdots \\ \cssId{mt-xe-sumk}{\sum_i x_{ik} e_i} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}\]

  • The first row: \(\sum_i e_i = 0\). The errors average zero.
  • Every other row: \(\sum_i x_{ij} e_i = 0\). With errors that average zero, this says the errors are uncorrelated with regressor \(j\).
  • Row \(j\) is the derivative for one coefficient, \(\partial SSE / \partial b_j = -2 \sum_i x_{ij} e_i\), set to zero.

OLS stops only when no regressor can explain anything left in the errors: if the errors still moved with the departure delay, a different \(b_1\) would cut the \(SSE\).

Check it in R

  • xe-sum0: The column of 1s times e: the sum of the errors. Zero means the errors average zero.
  • xe-sum1: Regressor 1 times e, added over flights. For the flights: Σ dep_delayᵢ × eᵢ. Zero means the errors do not move with the departure delay.
  • xe-sumk: The last regressor times e, added over flights. For the flights (k = 2): Σ sched_timeᵢ × eᵢ.

From the condition to the formula

\(\displaystyle {\color{#2471a3}{\mathbf{X}^\top}} \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}\)

Start from the condition At the OLS coefficients, the errors move with no regressor.

\(\displaystyle {\color{#2471a3}{\mathbf{X}^\top}} \left({\color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{y}}} - {\color{#2471a3}{\mathbf{X}}} \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\right) = \mathbf{0}\)

Plug in the errors At the minimum, \(\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\).

\(\displaystyle {\color{#2471a3}{\mathbf{X}^\top}} {\color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{y}}} - {\color{#2471a3}{\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}}}\, \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \mathbf{0}\)

Multiply out \(\mathbf{X}^\top\) multiplies each part of the bracket.

\(\displaystyle {\color{#2471a3}{\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}}}\, \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = {\color{#2471a3}{\mathbf{X}^\top}} {\color{#e67e22}{\mathbf{y}}}\)

Move one term across The normal equations: one equation per coefficient.

\(\displaystyle \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \boxed{\cssId{mt-xtxinv}{(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})^{-1}}\, \cssId{mt-xty}{\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{y}}}\)

Undo \(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\) Multiply both sides by its inverse, the matrix version of dividing. This is the formula on your formula sheet.

With one regressor and no intercept, \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} = \sum x_i^2\) and \(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} = \sum x_i y_i\), so \(\hat{\beta} = \sum x_i y_i / \sum x_i^2\): how \(x\) moves with \(y\), divided by how \(x\) moves with itself. With many regressors it is the same division, done with matrices. lm() does it for you.

Four flights, by hand See the matrices move

  • xtxinv: X’X holds how the regressors move together (sums of squares and cross-products). Its inverse untangles them, so each slope keeps only its own part.
  • xty: How each regressor moves with y.

Full rank: no column can be a copy

  • \(\mathbf{X}\) must have full rank: no column can be built exactly from the others.

Alone, a slope compares high x with low x. They differ in x, and in everything that travels with x.

Holding z fixed compares cases with the same z, so z no longer travels with x.

The rule behind it

Held fixed, inside the formula

Two regressors: \(x\) = PM2.5 and \(z\) = rain, both measured from their means, so the intercept drops out. Hover over any sum.

\(\displaystyle \hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\beta}_x \\ \hat{\beta}_z \end{bmatrix} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\,\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\)

The OLS formula The same formula as in section 1, with the columns \(x\) and \(z\).

\(\displaystyle \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} = \begin{bmatrix} \cssId{mt-hf-xx}{\sum x_i^2} & \cssId{mt-hf-xz}{{\color{#c0392b}{\sum x_i z_i}}} \\ \cssId{mt-hf-xz2}{{\color{#c0392b}{\sum x_i z_i}}} & \cssId{mt-hf-zz}{\sum z_i^2} \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} \cssId{mt-hf-xy}{\sum x_i y_i} \\ \cssId{mt-hf-zy}{\sum z_i y_i} \end{bmatrix}\)

What goes in Sums of squares and products. The red cross term: how \(x\) and \(z\) move together.

\(\displaystyle (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1} = \frac{1}{\cssId{mt-hf-det}{D}} \begin{bmatrix} \sum z_i^2 & \cssId{mt-hf-neg}{{\color{#c0392b}{-\sum x_i z_i}}} \\ {\color{#c0392b}{-\sum x_i z_i}} & \sum x_i^2 \end{bmatrix}\)

\(\displaystyle D = \sum x_i^2 \sum z_i^2 - \left(\sum x_i z_i\right)^2\)

Invert it The cross term comes back with a minus sign: the inverse discounts what \(x\) and \(z\) share.

\(\displaystyle \hat{\beta}_x = \frac{\cssId{mt-hf-top}{\sum z_i^2 \sum x_i y_i} - \cssId{mt-hf-sub}{{\color{#c0392b}{\sum x_i z_i \sum z_i y_i}}}}{D}\)

What it gives for \(x\) From the raw link of smog with trips, the formula takes out the part that runs through rain.

If \(x\) and \(z\) do not move together, \(\sum x_i z_i = 0\) and \(\hat{\beta}_x = \sum x_i y_i / \sum x_i^2\): the one-regressor slope of W07. Smog and rain do move together, so adding rain changed the smog slope.

Why it works: the part of \(x\) that \(z\) cannot predict

  • hf-xx: Σx²: how much smog varies from day to day. Each day’s PM2.5 minus the average, squared, added up. The more x varies, the more the data say about its slope.
  • hf-xz: Σxz: how smog and rain move together. Negative here: smoggy days tend to be dry (correlation -0.29). It would be zero if they were unrelated.
  • hf-xz2: Σxz: the same cross term. X’X is symmetric: smog with rain is rain with smog.
  • hf-zz: Σz²: how much rain varies from day to day.
  • hf-xy: Σxy: how smog moves with trips. This is the raw link, with the rain still mixed in.
  • hf-zy: Σzy: how rain moves with trips. Negative: wet days have fewer riders.
  • hf-det: D, the determinant: how much x and z vary apart from each other. If they always moved together, this would be zero and the inverse would not exist: X would not have full rank.
  • hf-neg: The cross term with a minus sign. This is where the formula discounts what smog and rain share.
  • hf-top: Smog’s raw link with trips, as if rain did not exist.
  • hf-sub: The part of smog’s link with trips that runs through rain: smog moves with rain (Σxz), and rain moves with trips (Σzy). The formula subtracts it.

Can random numbers raise R²?

Why R² can only rise

\(\displaystyle SSE_{\text{old}} = \min_{b_0,\, b_1,\, b_2} \sum_i \left( y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_{i1} - b_2 x_{i2} \right)^2\)

The old model OLS picks the smallest \(SSE\) with temperature and days off.

\(\displaystyle SSE_{\text{new}} = \min_{b_0, \dots, b_3} \sum_i \left( y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_{i1} - b_2 x_{i2} - b_3 x_{i3} \right)^2\)

Add the noise column One more coefficient to choose, \(b_3\).

\(\displaystyle b_3 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{the old model, with } SSE_{\text{old}}\)

One choice is to ignore it With its slope set to zero, the new column changes nothing.

\(\displaystyle \boxed{SSE_{\text{new}} \le SSE_{\text{old}} \;\Rightarrow\; R^2_{\text{new}} \ge R^2_{\text{old}}}\)

The best can only be as good or better OLS can always fall back on \(b_3 = 0\). \(SST\) does not change, so \(R^2 = 1 - SSE/SST\) cannot fall.

And it almost always rises. In a sample, random numbers are never exactly uncorrelated with the errors: in Exercise 9 the correlation of bikes$noise with the errors is \(-0.018\). Tiny, but OLS uses it to cut the \(SSE\) a little.

100 columns of random numbers

Take 120 Ecobici days, trips on temperature and days off. Add random columns, one at a time, up to 100.

  • Thin lines: 40 different sets of random columns. Thick line: their average.
  • \(R^2\) climbs from 0.45 to 0.92 with 100 columns of pure noise. With 117 columns it would reach 1: a perfect fit to noise.

Judged by \(R^2\), the model with 100 random columns is the best one. That makes no sense.

Each coefficient needs a fee: keep a regressor only if what it adds to the fit pays for the coefficient it uses.

Adjusted R²: a fee for each regressor

\[R^2 = 1 - \frac{SSE}{SST} \qquad\qquad \bar{R}^2 = 1 - \frac{\cssId{mt-sseadj}{SSE/(n-k-1)}}{\cssId{mt-sstadj}{SST/(n-1)}} = 1 - \frac{\cssId{mt-s2adj}{s^2}}{\cssId{mt-varyadj}{\text{var}(y)}}\]

  • sseadj: The unexplained variation per degree of freedom. Each new regressor uses one.
  • sstadj: The total variation per degree of freedom. It does not depend on the model.
  • s2adj: The variance of the errors, the residual standard error of summary() squared.
  • varyadj: The sample variance of the outcome, var(y) in R.
  • \(R^2\) looks only at \(SSE\), and every new coefficient lowers it.
  • Adjusted \(R^2\) divides \(SSE\) by \(n - k - 1\). Each new coefficient lowers \(n - k - 1\) by one: that is the fee.
  • A new regressor raises adjusted \(R^2\) only if it cuts \(SSE\) by more than the fee. This happens exactly when its \(|T|\) is above 1.
  • Adjusted \(R^2\) is no longer exactly the share of the variation explained: it is that share, adjusted for the number of regressors.
  • To compare models with different numbers of regressors, use adjusted \(R^2\).

All 781 Ecobici days, trips on temperature and days off: \(s\) = 3,690 trips and the standard deviation of trips is 5,548, so adjusted \(R^2\) is 1 − 3,690² / 5,548² = 0.558.

Appendix — the algebra of omitted-variable bias

True model: \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + u\). We fit \(y\) on \(x_1\) alone.

\(\displaystyle {\color{#2471a3}{x_2}} = \delta_0 + {\color{#2471a3}{\delta_1}} x_1 + v\)

Start Write the left-out regressor as a line in \(x_1\). \(\delta_1\) says how they move together.

\(\displaystyle y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 ({\color{#2471a3}{\delta_0 + \delta_1 x_1 + v}}) + u\)

Plug it in Replace \(x_2\) in the true model.

\(\displaystyle y = (\beta_0 + \beta_2 \delta_0) + ({\color{#c0392b}{\beta_1 + \beta_2 \delta_1}})\, x_1 + (\beta_2 v + u)\)

Collect the terms in \(x_1\) The slope on \(x_1\) alone is the red part.

\(\displaystyle \text{slope alone} = \boxed{\beta_1 + {\color{#c0392b}{\beta_2\,\delta_1}}}\)

Both conditions The bias \(\beta_2 \delta_1\) is zero if \(x_2\) does not move \(y\) (\(\beta_2 = 0\)) or does not move with \(x_1\) (\(\delta_1 = 0\)).

In the Ecobici data, with \(x_1\) = pm25 and \(x_2\) = rain, the identity holds exactly for the OLS estimates: \(-87.94 + (-180.37) \times (-0.1460) = -61.62\). With \(x_1\) = temp and \(x_2\) = dayoff: \(394.89 + (-15837.65) \times (-0.0208) = 723.55\). In the flights example, with \(x_1\) = sched_time and \(x_2\) = dep_delay: \(-0.0217 + 0.9215 \times 0.0161 = -0.0069\).

Appendix — the slope uses only what is new in x₁

Write \(x_1 = x\) and \(x_2 = z\), measured from their means, with \(S_{11} = \sum x_{i1}^2\), \(S_{12} = \sum x_{i1} x_{i2}\), \(S_{22} = \sum x_{i2}^2\), \(S_{1y} = \sum x_{i1} y_i\) and \(S_{2y} = \sum x_{i2} y_i\). The formula slide gives \(\hat{\beta}_1 = (S_{22} S_{1y} - S_{12} S_{2y}) / (S_{11} S_{22} - S_{12}^2)\).

\(\displaystyle \hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{S_{1y} - \cssId{mt-delta}{\delta}\, S_{2y}}{S_{11} - \delta\, S_{12}}, \qquad \delta = \frac{S_{12}}{S_{22}}\)

Divide top and bottom by \(S_{22}\) \(\delta\) is the slope of a regression of \(x_1\) on \(x_2\): how much \(x_1\) moves with \(x_2\).

\(\displaystyle \hat{\beta}_1 = \boxed{\frac{\sum \cssId{mt-ri}{r_i}\,(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum r_i^2}}\)

\(\displaystyle r_i = (x_{i1} - \bar{x}_1) - \delta\,(x_{i2} - \bar{x}_2)\)

Write the sums back out The top is \(\sum r_i (y_i - \bar{y})\). The bottom is \(\sum r_i^2\), because \(\delta^2 S_{22} = \delta S_{12}\). This is the W07 slope formula, with \(x_1\) replaced by \(r\).

\(r\) is the part of \(x_1\) that \(x_2\) cannot predict. Holding \(x_2\) fixed means: keep only that part of \(x_1\), and run the one-regressor regression of W07 on it.

  • delta: The slope of x₁ on x₂. Times x₂’s deviation, it is the part of x₁ that x₂ predicts.
  • ri: The residual of a regression of x₁ on x₂: what is left of x₁ once x₂ has predicted all it can. Its mean is zero.

Appendix — least squares by hand

Minimise \(SSE(b_0, b_1, b_2) = \sum (y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_{i1} - b_2 x_{i2})^2\) without matrices. As in W07, set each derivative to zero. First the intercept.

\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial SSE}{\partial b_0} = -2 \sum_i e_i = 0\)

\(\displaystyle e_i = y_i - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_{i1} - \hat{\beta}_2 x_{i2}\)

Derivative in \(b_0\) Each squared error changes by \(2e_i\) times \(-1\).

\(\displaystyle n\bar{y} - n\hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1\, n\bar{x}_1 - \hat{\beta}_2\, n\bar{x}_2 = 0\)

Sum each term \(\sum y_i = n\bar{y}\), \(\sum \hat{\beta}_0 = n\hat{\beta}_0\), \(\sum x_{i1} = n\bar{x}_1\), \(\sum x_{i2} = n\bar{x}_2\).

\(\displaystyle \hat{\beta}_0 = \bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}_1 - \hat{\beta}_2 \bar{x}_2\)

Divide by \(n\) The fitted plane passes through the point of means.

\(\displaystyle \boxed{SSE = \sum_i \left( \tilde{y}_i - b_1 \tilde{x}_{i1} - b_2 \tilde{x}_{i2} \right)^2}\)

Put \(\hat{\beta}_0\) back in With \(\tilde{y}_i = y_i - \bar{y}\), \(\tilde{x}_{i1} = x_{i1} - \bar{x}_1\), \(\tilde{x}_{i2} = x_{i2} - \bar{x}_2\): every variable measured from its mean. Two unknowns are left.

Write \(S_{11} = \sum \tilde{x}_{i1}^2\), \(S_{22} = \sum \tilde{x}_{i2}^2\), \(S_{12} = \sum \tilde{x}_{i1}\tilde{x}_{i2}\), \(S_{1y} = \sum \tilde{x}_{i1}\tilde{y}_i\), \(S_{2y} = \sum \tilde{x}_{i2}\tilde{y}_i\).

\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial SSE}{\partial b_1} = -2 \sum_i \tilde{x}_{i1}\, e_i = 0\)

\(\displaystyle e_i = \tilde{y}_i - \hat{\beta}_1 \tilde{x}_{i1} - \hat{\beta}_2 \tilde{x}_{i2}\)

Derivative in \(b_1\) Each squared error changes by \(2e_i\) times \(-\tilde{x}_{i1}\).

\(\displaystyle S_{11}\,\hat{\beta}_1 + {\color{#c0392b}{S_{12}}}\,\hat{\beta}_2 = S_{1y}\)

Split the sum, name each part \(\sum \tilde{x}_{i1}\tilde{y}_i - \hat{\beta}_1 \sum \tilde{x}_{i1}^2 - \hat{\beta}_2 \sum \tilde{x}_{i1}\tilde{x}_{i2} = 0\).

\(\displaystyle {\color{#c0392b}{S_{12}}}\,\hat{\beta}_1 + S_{22}\,\hat{\beta}_2 = S_{2y}\)

\(\displaystyle \left( S_{11} S_{22} - {\color{#c0392b}{S_{12}}}^2 \right) \hat{\beta}_1 = S_{22}\,S_{1y} - {\color{#c0392b}{S_{12}}}\,S_{2y}\)

Remove \(\hat{\beta}_2\) Multiply the first equation by \(S_{22}\) and the second by \(S_{12}\), then subtract.

\(\displaystyle \boxed{\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{S_{22}\,S_{1y} - {\color{#c0392b}{S_{12}}}\,S_{2y}}{S_{11}\,S_{22} - {\color{#c0392b}{S_{12}}}^2}}\)

Divide \(\hat{\beta}_2\) is the same with 1 and 2 swapped.

“Held fixed, inside the formula” reaches the same result from \((\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}\). If \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) do not move together, \(S_{12} = 0\) and \(\hat{\beta}_1 = S_{1y}/S_{11}\): the one-regressor slope of W07.

Check the sizes \(n \times (k+1)\) times \((k+1) \times 1\) gives \(n \times 1\): one number per flight.

One row times \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) Multiply each entry of the row by the matching \(\beta\), then add.

Appendix — what is left in the errors?

OLS has picked the coefficients of the regression of flights$arr_delay on flights$dep_delay and flights$sched_time. Can either regressor still explain part of the errors that are left?

  1. Fit the regression and save its residuals as e.
  2. Compute the mean of e.
  3. Compute the correlation of e with flights$dep_delay, and with flights$sched_time.
  4. What do the three numbers say?

residuals(reg) gives the residuals of a fitted regression reg.

reg <- lm(arr_delay ~ dep_delay + sched_time, data = flights)
e <- residuals(reg)

mean(e)
cor(e, flights$dep_delay)
cor(e, flights$sched_time)

All three are zero, up to rounding: R prints numbers like 1e-16. The errors average zero and move with neither regressor. Whatever the departure delay and the schedule can explain is already in the fitted line.
