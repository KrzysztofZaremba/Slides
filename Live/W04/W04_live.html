1 · Population, sample, parameter, statistic

The four words the rest of the course is written in.

Statistical language

  • Population: the entire group you want to learn about. Usually too big to measure.
    • Every Airbnb listing in Mexico City.
  • Parameter: one number describing the population. Fixed, unknown, never observed.
    • \(\cssId{mt-muc}{\mu_c}\) and \(\cssId{mt-mud}{\mu_d}\) — the mean price of every clean and every dirty apartment in the city. What you actually want is the gap \(\cssId{mt-mugap}{\mu_c - \mu_d}\).
  • Sample: the part of the population you actually have data on.
    • Your 200 listings — 100 clean, 100 dirty.
  • Statistic: one number computed from the sample. You can always calculate it, and it lands somewhere different every sample.
    • \(\cssId{mt-xbarc}{\bar{x}_c} = 1{,}245\) and \(\cssId{mt-xbard}{\bar{x}_d} = 869\), so the gap you measured is \(\cssId{mt-xgap}{377}\) pesos.

Parameter belongs to the population, statistic belongs to the sample. Greek letters (\(\mu\), \(\sigma\), \(\pi\)) for parameters; Latin letters or hats (\(\bar{x}\), \(s\), \(\hat{\pi}\)) for statistics.

  • A parameter does not have to be a mean. To know how many people in Mexico have diabetes, the population is everyone in Mexico and the parameter is a proportion:

\[\cssId{mt-pidiab}{\pi} = \frac{\cssId{mt-ndiab}{D}}{\cssId{mt-npop}{N}}\]

→ More situations to sort out

  • muc: The mean nightly price across every clean listing in Mexico City — not the mean of the clean listings in your sample. A fixed number you do not know.
  • mud: The same for every dirty listing. The business question is whether these two numbers differ, and by how much.
  • mugap: The number the decision actually turns on: how much more a clean apartment can charge across the whole city. Nobody has ever computed it.
  • xbarc: The mean price of the 100 clean listings in your sample. You can compute it today; it is not the same as mu_c.
  • xbard: The mean price of your 100 dirty listings.
  • xgap: Your estimate of the gap. It is one draw: a different 200 listings would hand you a different number.
  • pidiab: The population proportion — the share of the whole population with diabetes. A parameter, exactly like a population mean, just measured on a yes/no variable.
  • ndiab: How many people in the whole population have diabetes. Nobody has counted them.
  • npop: The size of the whole population. Dividing turns the count into a share, so the parameter sits between 0 and 1.

A parameter you cannot see, a statistic you can

  • The parameter \(\pi\) belongs to the population on the left: fixed, unknown, never observed.
  • The statistic \(\hat{\pi}\) — the share of diabetics among the people you actually surveyed — belongs to the sample on the right. It is a guess at the parameter, computed from the sample.
  • \(\hat{\pi}\) is what you can calculate; \(\pi\) is what you wanted. Everything from here on is about the gap between the two.

The same picture for a continuous variable

  • Top: every diner who sat down over a month, and the population mean \(\mu\) — the parameter.
  • Bottom: the 50 diners you actually timed, and the sample mean \(\bar{x}\) — the statistic.
  • Same shape, ragged instead of smooth, and the two dashed lines do not land in the same place. Draw a different 50 and the red line moves; the green one never does.

Why you almost never have the whole population

  • Checking everything costs more than the answer is worth. Decision: should we pay to rebuild the billing system? Parameter: \(\pi\), the share of all 200,000 invoices that contain an error. Checking all of them takes a team a month. Checking 300 takes an afternoon.
  • Measuring destroys the product. Decision: how long a warranty can we offer? Parameter: \(\mu\), the mean lifetime in hours of every battery we make. You only learn a battery’s lifetime by running it until it dies.
  • Most people never answer. Decision: do we keep the new subscription plan? Parameter: \(\mu\), the mean satisfaction score of all 40,000 subscribers. You email all of them; 600 reply.
  • The group has not happened yet. Decision: how much stock do we order for next month? Parameter: \(\mu\), mean daily demand next month. Next month’s customers do not exist yet. All you have is the past.

Every one of these is a number about a group you cannot measure, decided from a group you can. The question is always the same: how far from the truth can that number be?

2 · Drawing the sample

What has to be true of the draw for the guess to mean anything.

What is a random sample?

  • At random: each member of the population has an equal chance of being selected. Selecting is called drawing a sample.
  • Random variable \(P_i\): describes observation \(i\) before drawing — it could be any price from the distribution.
  • Random sample: a collection of random variables \(\{P_1, P_2, \dots, P_n\}\).
  • Observed value \(p_i\): once realized, it is a constant from our sample — no longer random.

IID samples

  • IID = Independent and Identically Distributed.
  • Independent: selecting one unit \(P_i\) doesn’t affect selecting another \(P_j\).
  • Identically distributed: all units \(P_i\) come from the same distribution.

3 · Estimators

The rule that turns a sample into a guess — and why it is random.

Estimators: intuition

  • Our method of guessing the parameter from the data.
  • A function of the random variables in the sample: \(\hat\theta = f(P_1, \dots, P_n)\).
  • Because it is random, we can analyze its statistical properties.
  • It cannot contain unknown quantities (like \(\sigma\) or \(\mu\)).

Estimators: ones we’ve seen

  • Sample mean: \(\hat{\mu_c} = \bar{P} = \frac{\sum_n P_i}{n}\)
  • Sample standard deviation: \(s_c = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (P_i - \bar{P})^2}\)
  • Each is a rule that turns a sample into a single guess.

Point estimate

  • A single number computed from the realized data \(\{p_1, \dots, p_n\}\): \[\cssId{mt-pbar}{\bar{p}} = f(p_1, \dots, p_n) = \frac{\cssId{mt-sumn}{\sum_n p_i}}{\cssId{mt-n}{n}}\]
  • No longer random — it is one number from one sample.
  • pbar: The point estimate — one number worked out from the one sample you actually drew. Lower-case p because these are realized data, not random variables any more.
  • sumn: Add up the values of the observations you have.
  • n: How many observations are in your sample.

  • We can’t say how close this one number is to the true mean without knowing the population.
  • But we can judge our method (the estimator) by looking at every estimate it could have produced.

4 · Judging an estimator

Bias, variance, and the trade-off between them.

What makes an estimator good?

The same parameter can be estimated in many different ways. For the average price you could take the sample mean, the median, the midpoint of the cheapest and dearest listing, or simply the first listing you happened to scrape. All of them are estimators.

So which ones are any good?

To judge a method rather than a single number, ask three things about its sampling distribution:

  1. Where is it centred? — its expectation \(\cssId{mt-expest}{E[\hat\theta]}\). If that sits on the parameter, the estimator is unbiased: it is right on average.

  2. How wide is it? — its variance \(\cssId{mt-varest}{Var(\hat\theta)}\). How often does a single estimate land close to that centre?

  3. What shape is it? — the whole distribution. Only the shape lets you attach confidence to a number: “we are 95% confident the true mean is within 100 pesos of our estimate”. That is next week’s business.

  • expest: The average estimate over every sample you could have drawn. Not the average of your data — the average of the estimator itself, across samples that never happened.
  • varest: How far the estimates spread around that average. A small variance means almost any sample you draw hands you nearly the same answer.

Centre, spread, shape — we take them in that order.

The expectation of an estimator

Three symbols that are easy to confuse.

  • \(\theta\) — the parameter. One fixed, unknown number about the population: the average price of every listing in the city, or the clean-minus-dirty gap. It never moves, and you never see it.

  • \(\hat\theta\) — the estimator. The rule you apply to a sample. It is random: draw a different sample and it hands you a different number.

  • \(E[\hat\theta]\) — the expectation of the estimator. Take every sample you could have drawn, apply the rule to each, and average all those answers.

\(E[\hat\theta]\) is not the average of your data. It is the centre of the sampling distribution — the average of numbers you never computed, from samples you never drew.

Bias: is the rule right on average?

\[\cssId{mt-bias}{Bias(\hat\theta)} = \cssId{mt-Ehat}{E[\hat\theta]} - \cssId{mt-theta}{\theta}\]

  • bias: Bias is a property of the METHOD, not of one estimate. Zero bias means the recipe lands on the truth on average — it does not mean your particular sample did.
  • Ehat: The average value the estimator takes across all the samples you could have drawn — the centre of its sampling distribution. Not the number your one sample gave you.
  • theta: The true parameter: a fixed, unknown number about the population. You never observe it, which is why bias can only be reasoned about, never measured from data.
  • An estimator is unbiased when \(E[\hat\theta] = \theta\) — for any value \(\theta\) might take. Its sampling distribution is centred exactly on the parameter.
  • That is a statement about the rule, not about your sample. An unbiased rule can still hand you a number far from \(\theta\); bias is about the centre, not the width.
  • To see it: take many samples, compute the estimator in each, and average those values — does that average land on the parameter?
  • In practice you have one sample, so you can never actually do that.
  • But you can usually derive \(E[\hat\theta]\) on paper, with no data at all. That is what the next few slides do.

Three candidate estimators

Same job: guess \(\mu\) from an IID sample.

  • \(\hat\theta_1 = \frac{3X_1 + X_2}{4}\) — leans almost entirely on the first observation.
  • \(\hat\theta_2 = \frac{3X_1 + X_2}{5}\) — the same idea with different weights.
  • \(\hat\theta_3 = \bar X = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i}{n}\) — the sample mean, whatever \(n\) happens to be.

Which of them are unbiased? And is unbiased the same as good?

Example 1: \(\hat\theta_1 = \frac{3X_1 + X_2}{4}\)

  • Every \(X_i\) has the same unknown mean \(E(X_i) = \mu\).
  • Expected value: \(E(\hat\theta_1) = \tfrac{3}{4}\mu + \tfrac{1}{4}\mu = \mu\).
  • Bias: \(\mu - \mu = 0\) → unbiased. The weights add to 1, which is all that is needed.
  • But it throws away \(X_3\) and \(X_4\) entirely. Is it a good estimator? (We come back to this.)

Example 2: \(\hat\theta_2 = (3X_1 + X_2)/5\)

  • Same setup: \(E(X_i) = \mu\).
  • Expected value: \(E(\hat\theta_2) = \tfrac{3}{5}\mu + \tfrac{1}{5}\mu = \tfrac{4}{5}\mu\).
  • Bias: \(\tfrac{4}{5}\mu - \mu = -\tfrac{1}{5}\mu\) → biased (weights don’t sum to 1).

Example 3: \(\hat\theta_3 = \bar{X} = \frac{\sum X_i}{n}\) (the sample mean)

  • Same setup: \(E(X_i) = \mu\). Every observation gets the same weight \(\tfrac{1}{n}\).
  • Expected value: \(E(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sum_n E(X_i)}{n} = \frac{n\mu}{n} = \mu\).
  • Bias: \(0\) → unbiased. The sample mean is our workhorse estimator.
  • Look at what that proof needed: only that each \(E(X_i) = \mu\). We never had to know what \(\mu\) is — and we never will.
  • That is exactly why it is useful. Whatever the true average price turns out to be, the sample mean is aimed at it — so the number your one sample hands you is real information about \(\mu\).

Two unbiased estimators — is one of them better?

So we want an estimator that is unbiased. That rules out \(\hat\theta_2\) — but it left two survivors, and it will often leave more than one. How do we choose between them?

The two survivors are \(\hat\theta_1\) and \(\hat\theta_3\). To compare them concretely, take a sample of four observations, so the sample mean is \(\bar X = \frac{X_1+X_2+X_3+X_4}{4}\):

  • \(\hat\theta_1 = \frac{3X_1 + X_2}{4}\) — three quarters of the weight on one observation.
  • \(\hat\theta_3 = \frac{X_1+X_2+X_3+X_4}{4}\) — the weight spread evenly over all four.

The weights sum to 1 in both, so both are unbiased. Bias cannot separate them.

Which one would you rather report — and what evidence would settle it?

Two unbiased estimators, 5,000 samples each

Each estimator computed in 5,000 samples of four draws from \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(10, 10)\).

  • Both histograms are centred on the true value — unbiased, exactly as the algebra said.
  • But \(\hat\theta_1\)’s estimates are spread much wider. Plenty of them land 5 or more away from the truth; \(\hat\theta_3\)’s almost never do.
  • You only ever draw one sample. A wide estimator is right on average and can still hand you an answer far from \(\mu\) — and you would never know.
  • So among unbiased estimators, the one whose estimates cluster tightest is the one you want. It is called the more efficient estimator.

To rank them we need a number for that spread — the variance of the estimator.

Variance of an estimator: the definition

\[\cssId{mt-varhat}{Var(\hat\theta)} = \cssId{mt-Eavg}{E}\big[\cssId{mt-devcentre}{(\hat\theta - E[\hat\theta])}^2\big]\]

  • varhat: The variance of the estimator: how much your answer would jump around if you redrew the sample. It says nothing about how spread out the data themselves are.
  • Eavg: Average taken over every sample you could have drawn, not over the observations in your one sample.
  • devcentre: How far one possible estimate falls from the estimator’s own average — measured against E of theta-hat, not against the true parameter. Squared, so misses in both directions count and big misses count much more.

Two rules turn that definition into numbers:

  • a constant comes out squared: \(Var(aX) = a^2 Var(X)\)
  • for independent draws the variance of a sum is the sum of the variances.

Apply them to each candidate and the numbers fall straight out.

Variance: worked examples

All three estimate the same \(\mu\) from the same four draws. Only the weights differ.

Estimator Weights Variance In units of \(\sigma^2\)
\(X_1\) alone \(1,\,0,\,0,\,0\) \(\sigma^2\) \(1.00\)
\(\hat\theta_1 = \dfrac{3X_1+X_2}{4}\) \(\tfrac34,\,\tfrac14,\,0,\,0\) \(\dfrac{9\sigma^2+\sigma^2}{16}=\dfrac{10\sigma^2}{16}\) \(0.63\)
\(\hat\theta_3 = \bar X\) \(\tfrac14,\,\tfrac14,\,\tfrac14,\,\tfrac14\) \(\dfrac{\sigma^2}{4}=\dfrac{4\sigma^2}{16}\) \(\mathbf{0.25}\)

The more evenly the weight is spread, the smaller the variance. Spread it as evenly as possible and you get the sample mean:

\[Var(\bar X) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\]

It falls with every observation you add — which is exactly what the uneven weights give up.

Relative efficiency

Among unbiased estimators, the smaller variance wins:

\[\cssId{mt-varA}{Var(\hat\theta_1)} = \tfrac{10\sigma^2}{16} \;>\; \tfrac{4\sigma^2}{16} = \cssId{mt-varB}{Var(\hat\theta_3)}\]

  • varA: Theta-hat-1 loads three quarters of its weight onto a single observation, so one unlucky draw moves it a lot — a bigger variance.
  • varB: The sample mean spreads the weight evenly over four observations, so their errors partly cancel and the estimate is steadier.

\[Eff(\hat\theta_3, \hat\theta_1) = \frac{Var(\hat\theta_1)}{Var(\hat\theta_3)} = \frac{10}{4} = 2.5\]

The sample mean is 2.5× as efficient.

Side note: what if variables aren’t independent?

So far we assumed independence. Suppose \(X_1, X_2\) are not independent (e.g. daily sales of two products in one store).

  • \(E(X_1 + X_2) = E(X_1) + E(X_2)\) (always true).
  • \(Var(X_1 + X_2) = Var(X_1) + Var(X_2) + 2\,Cov(X_1, X_2)\)
  • \(Var(X_1 - X_2) = Var(X_1) + Var(X_2) - 2\,Cov(X_1, X_2)\)

Mean Squared Error (MSE)

One number for how good an estimator is — how far it lands from the truth, on average:

\[\cssId{mt-mse}{MSE(\hat\theta)} = \cssId{mt-EavgM}{E}\big[\cssId{mt-errtrue}{(\hat\theta - \theta)}^2\big]\]

  • mse: Mean squared error — one number that puts together how far off the estimator is on average and how much it bounces around.
  • EavgM: Average taken over every sample you could have drawn, not over the observations in your one sample.
  • errtrue: The miss from the TRUE parameter. Compare with variance, which measures distance from the estimator’s own average — that one difference is what separates the two ideas. Squared, so overshooting and undershooting both count.

It splits into exactly two pieces, and nothing is left over:

\[MSE(\hat\theta) = \cssId{mt-varterm}{\text{Var}(\hat\theta)} \;+\; \cssId{mt-biasterm}{\text{Bias}(\hat\theta)^2}\]

  • varterm: The variance part — how much the estimator bounces from sample to sample. This is the part that shrinks as you collect more data.
  • biasterm: The bias part, squared — how far off the estimator is on average. This part does NOT shrink with n: more data never repairs a biased method.
  • Variance — how much your answer moves when the sample changes.
  • Bias² — how far the method is off on average.
  • If the estimator is unbiased, the second term is zero and \(MSE(\hat\theta) = Var(\hat\theta)\).

→ The two-line derivation

A lower MSE can mean a biased estimator

Nothing says the winner on MSE has to be unbiased. A method that is slightly off but very steady can beat one that is right on average but jumps around.

  • MSE is one suggestion of which estimator is better — it weighs bias and variance against each other in a particular way.
  • It is not a law. Whether you would rather be unbiased or have low variance depends on what the number is for.

5 · The Central Limit Theorem

Centre and spread are settled. What is the shape?

From sampling distribution to the CLT

  • We know the mean and variance of the sample mean. Can we say anything about its whole distribution?
  • For the sample mean — yes!
  • That’s the Central Limit Theorem, the most exciting theorem in statistics.

CLT: the setup

Take \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) i.i.d. with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\).

The theorem talks about three quantities, and they are the same thing rescaled:

Definition Centre Spread
Sum \(S_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i\) \(n\mu\) \(\sqrt{n}\,\sigma\)
Mean \(\bar X_n = \dfrac{S_n}{n}\) \(\mu\) \(\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\)
Standardized \(Z_n = \dfrac{\bar X_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\) \(0\) \(1\)
  • Divide the sum by \(n\) and you get the mean: the centre drops from \(n\mu\) to \(\mu\) and the spread from \(\sqrt{n}\sigma\) to \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\). Sums get more variable with \(n\); averages get less.
  • Subtract the centre and divide by the spread and you get \(Z_n\) — the same quantity with the units stripped off.

That \(\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\) has a name: the standard error of the mean. It is the standard deviation of \(\bar X_n\), not of the data.

CLT: the theorem

For large \(n\):

\[\small \cssId{mt-Sn}{S_n} \mathrel{\cssId{mt-approx}{\dot\sim}} \mathcal{N}\!\left(n\mu,\,\sqrt{n}\,\sigma\right), \quad \cssId{mt-Xbarn}{\bar{X}_n} \mathrel{\dot\sim} \mathcal{N}\!\left(\cssId{mt-mu}{\mu},\,\cssId{mt-se}{\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}}\right), \quad \cssId{mt-Zn}{Z_n} \mathrel{\dot\sim} \cssId{mt-stdnorm}{\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\]

  • In large samples \(\bar{X}_n\) is approximately normal, centered at \(\mu\), with st. dev. \(\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\).
  • As \(n\) grows, the approximation improves and the spread shrinks with \(\sqrt{n}\).
  • Sn: The total of all n observations, not the average. Sums get more variable as n grows; averages get less variable.
  • approx: “Approximately distributed as”. The CLT is an approximation that keeps improving as n grows, not an exact law for a fixed n.
  • Xbarn: The sample mean of n draws, treated as a random variable — draw a different sample and you get a different value.
  • mu: The population mean — the true average in the whole population. Fixed, unknown, and what you are trying to learn.
  • se: The standard error: the standard deviation of the sample MEAN, not of the data. It measures how much the mean moves from sample to sample. It falls with the square root of n, so four times the data halves it.
  • Zn: The standardised sample mean: subtract where it is centred and divide by its own standard error. Whatever the original units, the result is on the same z scale.
  • stdnorm: The standard normal — mean 0, standard deviation 1. Once you standardise, one table (or qnorm) answers every problem.

What if I don’t know \(\sigma\)?

  • By the CLT, \(\bar{X} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(200, \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{80}}\right)\) — but \(\sigma\) is unknown.

  • That adds a little uncertainty, but in a large sample you estimate \(\sigma\) well.

Where \(Var(\bar X) = \sigma^2/n\) comes from

Two rules do all the work: a constant comes out of a variance squared, and the variance of a sum is the sum of the variances plus every covariance.

\[Var(\bar X) = Var\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i\right) = \frac{1}{n^2}\,Var\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i\right)\]

\[Var\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} Var(X_i) \; + \underbrace{\sum_{i \neq j} Cov(X_i, X_j)}_{\text{every pair, twice}}\]

Now use independence. If the listings are drawn independently, every \(Cov(X_i, X_j)\) with \(i \neq j\) is zero, and the whole second term disappears:

\[Var\!\left(\sum X_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sigma^2 = n\sigma^2\]

\[Var(\bar X) = \frac{1}{n^2}\cdot n\sigma^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\]

So the \(\sqrt{n}\) law is not free. It is bought entirely with the independence assumption — see what happens without it.

Where \(\frac{10\sigma^2}{16}\) comes from

Same two rules, applied to \(\hat\theta_1 = \frac{3X_1 + X_2}{4}\).

\[Var(\hat\theta_1) = Var\!\left(\frac{3X_1 + X_2}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{16}\,Var(3X_1 + X_2)\]

\[= \frac{1}{16}\Big[\,9\,Var(X_1) + Var(X_2) + 2\cdot 3\cdot Cov(X_1, X_2)\,\Big]\]

Independent draws, so the covariance is zero and each variance is \(\sigma^2\):

\[= \frac{9\sigma^2 + \sigma^2}{16} = \frac{10\sigma^2}{16}\]

The \(9\) is where the damage is done: tripling the weight on \(X_1\) multiplies its contribution by nine, and nothing else is there to offset it.

Where MSE = Var + Bias² comes from

Add and subtract \(E(\hat\theta)\) inside the square, then expand.

\[\begin{align*} MSE(\hat{\theta}) &= E[(\hat{\theta}-\theta)^2] = E\big[\big(\hat{\theta}-E(\hat{\theta})+E(\hat{\theta})-\theta\big)^2\big]\\[4pt] &= E[(\hat{\theta} - E(\hat{\theta}))^2] + 2\,\cssId{mt-crossterm}{\underbrace{E[\hat{\theta} - E(\hat{\theta})]}_{=\,0}}\,(E(\hat{\theta}) - \theta) + (E(\hat{\theta}) - \theta)^2 \\[4pt] &= \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) + \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta})^2 \end{align*}\]

  • crossterm: This middle term is zero: on average an estimator deviates from its own mean by nothing at all. That is why the MSE splits into exactly two pieces with no leftover.

The trick is the middle line: the cross term vanishes because an estimator’s average deviation from its own average is zero, by definition.

What if the draws are not independent?

Start with two. The variance of a sum is not just the sum of the variances:

\[Var(X_1 + X_2) = Var(X_1) + Var(X_2) + 2\,Cov(X_1, X_2)\]

\[Var(X_1 - X_2) = Var(X_1) + Var(X_2) - 2\,Cov(X_1, X_2)\]

With \(n\) of them the same thing happens for every pair:

\[Var\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} Var(X_i) \;+\; \sum_{i \neq j} Cov(X_i, X_j)\]

so the sample mean picks them up too:

\[Var(\bar X) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \;+\; \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i \neq j} Cov(X_i, X_j)\]

Positive covariance makes it bigger than \(\sigma^2/n\); negative covariance makes it smaller. Independence sets every one of those terms to zero.

Normal distribution: combining variables

A customer who opens the DiDi app may call a car. \(X\) = calls in Cancún (Bernoulli 0.4), \(Y\) = in Puerto Vallarta (Bernoulli 0.6). Sample: 100 from Cancún, 80 from PV. P(more than 100 people call a car)?

Reminders (for normal \(X\)): \[X + c \sim \mathcal{N}(\cssId{mt-shift}{\mu + c}, \cssId{mt-shiftsd}{\sigma}), \qquad cX \sim \mathcal{N}(c\mu, \cssId{mt-scalesd}{|c|\sigma})\] \[X + Y \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \cssId{mt-sumsd}{\sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}}\right)\]

  • shift: Adding a constant slides the whole distribution along — the centre moves by exactly c.
  • shiftsd: The standard deviation does not change. Sliding a distribution sideways does not stretch it, so the spread is untouched.
  • scalesd: Multiplying by c stretches the spread by the size of c. Absolute value, because a standard deviation can never be negative.
  • sumsd: Standard deviations do NOT add. Variances add, so you sum the squares and take the square root at the very end — and only when X and Y are independent.

🖊️ Exercise 11 — Which ones are unbiased?

The code is written for you: it draws 5,000 samples of n = 10, computes all three estimators in each one, and reports two rows — the bias of each estimator (its average minus the parameter) and how spread out each one is.

Press Run, then answer from the output:

  1. Which of the three are unbiased?
  2. Among those, which one would you rather use — and why?

Read the first row: a bias of 0 means the estimator lands on the parameter on average. Then read the second row for the ones that passed.

mu <- mean(population)

est <- replicate(5000, {
  x <- sample(population, 10)
  c(theta1 = x[1],
    theta2 = (3 * x[1] + x[2]) / 5,
    theta3 = mean(x))
})

round(rbind(bias   = rowMeans(est) - mu,
            spread = apply(est, 1, sd)), 1)
\(\hat\theta_1 = X_1\) \(\hat\theta_2 = \frac{3X_1 + X_2}{5}\) \(\hat\theta_3 = \bar{X}\)
bias ≈ 0 −229 ≈ 0
spread 1,032 652 323

1. \(\hat\theta_1\) and \(\hat\theta_3\) are unbiased. \(\hat\theta_2\) sits about \(-\mu/5 = -234\) pesos below the truth every time, because its weights add to \(4/5\) instead of 1 — it is systematically too low, and more data will not fix it.

2. \(\hat\theta_3\), the sample mean. Both are right on average, but \(\hat\theta_1\) has a spread of 1,032 — the population’s own standard deviation, because it throws away 9 of the 10 listings. The sample mean’s spread is \(\sigma/\sqrt{10} = 323\), three times tighter.

Unbiased is not the same as good. \(\hat\theta_1\) is unbiased and useless.

🖊️ Exercise 12 — Which unbiased estimator wins?

Simulate 1,000 samples of four draws from \(\mathcal{N}(10, 10)\), compute both estimators in each sample, then produce the evidence: their averages, their standard deviations, and the two histograms side by side.

matrix(rnorm(4 * 1000, 10, 10), ncol = 4) gives 1,000 samples, one per row. d[, 1] is the first column, rowMeans(d) averages each row. par(mfrow = c(1, 2)) puts two hist() calls side by side.

set.seed(123); n <- 1000

📝 Exam practice 1 — Liga MX winning percentages

Liga MX teams play 34 games per season. A team’s final winning percentage is its number of wins divided by 34. Assume each match is an i.i.d. Bernoulli trial with the team’s “true” win probability. [I1]

  1. If Club América has win probability \(\pi_A = 0.58\), what is the asymptotic distribution of América’s winning percentage \(W_A\)?
  2. If América has \(\pi_A = 0.58\) and Pumas has \(\pi_B = 0.52\), what is the asymptotic distribution of \(W_A - W_B\), assuming independence?
  3. Based on (b), what is \(P(W_B > W_A)\)?

pnorm(q, mean, sd) returns \(P(X \le q)\) for a normal. sqrt() turns a variance into a standard deviation — pnorm() wants the standard deviation, not the variance.

\(W_A = \bar X\) of 34 Bernoulli trials, so by the CLT \(W_A \mathrel{\dot\sim} N\!\left(\pi_A,\ \frac{\pi_A(1-\pi_A)}{34}\right)\); for the difference the variances add (independence); and \(P(W_B > W_A) = P(W_A - W_B < 0)\).

n   <- 34
piA <- 0.58; piB <- 0.52

# (a) W_A ~ N(0.58, 0.00717), SE = 0.0846
var_A <- piA * (1 - piA) / n
c(mean = piA, var = var_A, se = sqrt(var_A))

# (b) W_A - W_B ~ N(0.06, 0.01451), SE = 0.1204
var_B    <- piB * (1 - piB) / n
var_diff <- var_A + var_B
c(mean = piA - piB, var = var_diff, se = sqrt(var_diff))

# (c) P(W_B > W_A) = P(W_A - W_B < 0) = P(Z < -0.498) = 0.3092
pnorm(0, mean = piA - piB, sd = sqrt(var_diff))

There is about a 31% chance that Pumas finishes with a better record than América, even though América has the higher true win probability.

📝 Exam practice 2 — Factory injuries (Poisson)

In a factory in Monterrey, the number of workplace injuries is recorded weekly for 240 weeks. The average number of injuries per week is 0.6, with \(X_t \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)\). [I2]

  1. Provide an asymptotic 90% confidence interval for \(\lambda\).

qnorm(0.95) is the z-value for a two-sided 90% interval. x + c(-1, 1) * m builds both endpoints of an interval in one line.

For a Poisson distribution \(\text{Var}(X) = \lambda\), so \(SE(\hat\lambda) = SE(\bar X) = \sqrt{\hat\lambda / n}\), and the asymptotic 90% CI uses \(z_{0.05} = 1.645\).

lambda_hat <- 0.6
n          <- 240
se <- sqrt(lambda_hat / n)              # 0.0500
lambda_hat + c(-1, 1) * 1.645 * se      # (0.518, 0.682)

\(\hat\lambda \pm 1.645 \times SE = 0.6 \pm 0.0822 = (0.518,\ 0.682)\).

📝 Exam practice 3 — Unemployment duration: concepts

A random sample of 500 unemployed workers in Guadalajara is surveyed about unemployment duration in weeks, \(X\). By the CLT, \(\bar{X}\) is approximately normal. [I8]

  1. If \(P(\bar{X} > 20) = 0.5\), what is \(E(X)\)?
  2. If \(P(\bar{X} > 20) = 0.4\), what can be said about \(E(X)\)?

Answer them by finding the mean that produces each probability. Take \(\sigma_X = 10.5\), so \(SE = 10.5/\sqrt{500}\).

qnorm(p, mean, sd) inverts pnorm(): it returns the value with probability p below it. So P(X > 20) = p says 20 is the 1 - p quantile of \(\bar X\) — write that as a qnorm() equation and solve it for the mean.

se <- 10.5 / sqrt(500)
c(a = 20 - qnorm(0.50) * se,      # 20.00
  b = 20 - qnorm(0.60) * se)      # 19.88  -> below 20

(a) \(E(X) = 20\) — if half the distribution of \(\bar X\) lies above 20, then 20 is its median; the normal distribution is symmetric, so median \(=\) mean.

(b) \(E(X) < 20\) — now \(P(\bar X \le 20) = 0.6 > 0.5\), so more than half the distribution sits below 20, which means the mean is below 20. On the exam, the sign is the whole answer — no computation needed.

📝 Exam practice 3 — Unemployment duration: calculations

Same setup: \(n = 500\) workers, \(\bar X\) approximately normal by the CLT. [I8]

  1. If \(E(X) = 19.5\) and \(\sigma_X = 10.5\), what is \(P(\bar{X} > 20)\)?
  2. How would the answer to (c) change if \(n = 1600\) instead of \(n = 500\)?

pnorm(q, mean, sd) gives \(P(X \le q)\), so 1 - pnorm(...) gives the upper tail. The sd argument here describes \(\bar X\), not one worker.

Standardize with the standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), not \(\sigma\):

mu    <- 19.5
sigma <- 10.5

# (c) SE = 10.5/sqrt(500) = 0.4696 -> P(Z > 1.065) = 0.144
se_500 <- sigma / sqrt(500)
1 - pnorm(20, mean = mu, sd = se_500)

# (d) SE = 10.5/sqrt(1600) = 0.2625 -> P(Z > 1.905) = 0.028
se_1600 <- sigma / sqrt(1600)
1 - pnorm(20, mean = mu, sd = se_1600)

The probability decreases substantially: the larger sample shrinks the standard error, so the sample mean is less likely to deviate from the true mean.

📝 Exam practice 4 — Cereal box weights

A cereal company in Guadalajara sells 500g boxes. Box weights follow \(X \sim N(500, 9)\) (variance 9, so \(\sigma = 3\)). Boxes weighing less than 496g must be repackaged. [I9]

  1. What is the probability that a box is repackaged?
  2. For 15,000 boxes, what is the approximate (normal) distribution of the number repackaged?

pnorm(q, mean, sd) gives \(P(X \le q)\). Part (a) is about a single box, so the sd argument is the one given in the problem. In (b), the count of repackaged boxes among 15,000 independent boxes is a Binomial — recall its mean and variance.

# (a) P(X < 496) = P(Z < -1.333) = 0.0912
p <- pnorm(496, mean = 500, sd = 3)
p

# (b) Y ~ Binom(15000, 0.0912); normal approximation:
n <- 15000
c(mean = n * p,                       # 1368.2
  var  = n * p * (1 - p),             # 1243.4
  sd   = sqrt(n * p * (1 - p)))       # 35.3

About 9.1% of boxes need repackaging, and \(Y \mathrel{\dot\sim} N(1368,\ 1243)\), i.e. SD \(\approx 35.3\).

(c) Profits per box are \(P_1\) if at least 496g and \(P_2\) if less. For 15,000 boxes, what is the approximate distribution of total profits?

Total profit is \(\Pi = (15000 - Y)P_1 + Y P_2 = 15000\,P_1 - Y(P_1 - P_2)\). With \(a = P_1 - P_2\), a linear function of the (approximately normal) \(Y\) is normal: \[\cssId{mt-Pi}{\Pi} \mathrel{\dot\sim} N\!\left(\cssId{mt-profmean}{15000\,P_1 - 1368.2\,a},\ \ \cssId{mt-profvar}{1243.4\,a^2}\right)\]

  • Pi: Total profit across all 15,000 boxes. It is random only because the number of underweight boxes is random — the prices themselves are fixed.
  • profmean: Expected profit: full profit on every box, minus the 1368.2 boxes you expect to be underweight times the profit lost on each.
  • profvar: This slot holds a VARIANCE, not a standard deviation. Multiplying a random variable by the constant a multiplies its variance by a squared, so the constant comes out squared here.

📝 Exam practice 5 — Online marketplace visitors

The number of visitors \(X\) to a Mexican online marketplace during one minute is \(\text{Poisson}(\lambda = 150)\). [I10]

  1. Thinking of \(X\) as the sum of 60 i.i.d. Poisson(2.5) random variables, what is the asymptotic normal distribution associated with \(X\)?
  2. Provide an approximate 90% probability interval for \(X\) using the asymptotic distribution.
  3. Calculate the exact probability (based on \(\text{Poisson}(150)\)) that \(X\) is within the interval from (b).

qnorm(0.95) is the two-sided 90% z-value; sqrt() converts variance to standard deviation. In (c), ppois(k, lambda) is \(P(X \le k)\) — visitors are whole numbers, so round the endpoints inward before using it.

By the CLT for a sum: \(E[X] = 60 \times 2.5 = 150\) and \(\text{Var}(X) = 60 \times 2.5 = 150\), so \(X \mathrel{\dot\sim} N(150, 150)\) with SD \(= \sqrt{150} \approx 12.25\).

lambda <- 150
sd_X   <- sqrt(lambda)                        # (a) X ~ N(150, 150), SD = 12.25

# (b) 90% interval: 150 +/- 1.645 * 12.25 = (129.85, 170.15)
lambda + c(-1, 1) * 1.645 * sd_X

# (c) exact Poisson probability of landing in (129.85, 170.15)
ppois(170, 150) - ppois(129, 150)             # 0.9061

The exact probability is 0.906, very close to the nominal 90% — the normal approximation works well for \(\lambda = 150\).

Bonus: why is \(S = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i\) an unbiased estimator of \(n\mu_X\)?

\(E[S] = E\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right] = \sum_{i=1}^n E[X_i] = n\mu_X\) — the expected value of \(S\) equals the parameter, which is exactly the definition of unbiasedness.

📝 Exam practice 6 — Franchise revenues

You own 60 taquería franchises and 30 torta franchises. Monthly revenue (thousands of pesos) for each franchise is i.i.d. with: [I14]

  • Taquerías: \(\mu = 22\), \(\sigma = 5\)
  • Tortas: \(\mu = 17\), \(\sigma = 4\)
  1. What is the approximate distribution of the sample average of revenues for the 30 torta franchises?
  2. What is the approximate distribution of the sample average of revenues across all 90 franchises?

For (b), write the overall average as a weighted sum of the two group averages, (60 * xbar_T + 30 * xbar_R) / 90, and then apply the rules for the mean and the variance of a weighted sum of independent variables.

Part (a) is the plain CLT; part (b) is a weighted average of two independent sample means, so take the weighted mean and combine variances with squared weights.

# (a) torta average: N(17, 16/30), SD = 0.730
c(mean = 17, var = 4^2 / 30, sd = sqrt(4^2 / 30))

# (b) overall average = (60*xbar_T + 30*xbar_R) / 90
mu_all  <- (60 * 22 + 30 * 17) / 90                       # 20.333
var_all <- (60^2 * (5^2 / 60) + 30^2 * (4^2 / 30)) / 90^2 # 0.2444
c(mean = mu_all, var = var_all, sd = sqrt(var_all))

\(\bar X_{\text{all}} \mathrel{\dot\sim} N(20.33,\ 0.244)\), i.e. SD \(\approx 0.494\).
