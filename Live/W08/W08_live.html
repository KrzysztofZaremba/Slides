The slope is a random variable

  • \(\hat{\beta}_0\) and \(\hat{\beta}_1\) are estimators: rules that turn a sample into a number.
  • So they are random variables. Their values depend on which sample we happened to draw.
  • That raises the two questions every estimator must answer:
    • Is it right on average? That is, is it unbiased?
    • How much does it move from sample to sample? That is, how big is its variance?

The sampler’s histogram is the sampling distribution of \(\hat{\beta}_1\). Its centre and its spread are the next two sections.

2 · How much does the slope move?

Centre first, then spread. The spread has a name: the standard error.

Right on average

Under the four assumptions from last week:

  1. linear in the parameters, with an additive error;
  2. \(E(u_i) = 0\);
  3. \(\text{Var}(u_i) = \sigma^2\);
  4. \(\text{Cov}(u_i, u_j) = 0\) for \(i \neq j\);

OLS is unbiased:

\[\cssId{mt-Eb1}{E(\hat{\beta}_1)} = E\left(\frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}\right) = \beta_1 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \cssId{mt-Eb0}{E(\hat{\beta}_0)} = \beta_0\]

  • Eb1: The average of the slope over all possible samples. Your histogram of 1,000 slopes was centred here.
  • Eb0: The same for the intercept: over many samples, the intercept is right on average.

Assumption 2 alone is enough for unbiasedness. Assumptions 3 and 4 matter for the spread.

When is the slope biased?

A firm regresses employees’ salary on their years of education, to learn what one more year of schooling is worth.

  • What kinds of things end up in the error \(u_i\)?
  • Are they related to education?
  • Is the slope unbiased?

Ability, motivation, family connections and the quality of the school all move salary and all sit in \(u_i\).

People with more of them also tend to study longer. Then \(u_i\) is higher, on average, for people with more education: \(E(u_i)\) is not zero at every level of \(x\).

The slope is then biased upward. It mixes the effect of schooling with the effect of everything that comes with schooling.

Three things that could make the slope steadier

On the sampler, a typical slope missed the true slope by about 0.5. Which of these would make that number smaller?

  • more points in each sample?
  • less scatter around the line?
  • more spread in \(x\)?

Go back to the sampler and move one slider at a time.

What the sliders showed

  • Raise \(n\): the histogram gets narrower. More points pin the line down.
  • Raise the noise \(\sigma\): the histogram gets wider. More scatter lets many slopes fit.
  • Raise the spread of \(x\): the histogram gets narrower. Points far apart in \(x\) fix the tilt of the line, like a long plank is easier to level than a short one.
  • In every setting the histogram stays centred on 3. The spread changes; the centre does not.

The variance of the slope

All three levers sit in one formula:

\[\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\cssId{mt-sig2}{\sigma^2}}{\cssId{mt-sxx}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}} = \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}}\]

  • sig2: The variance of the error — how widely the points scatter around the true line. More noise, bigger variance.
  • sxx: The total squared spread of x around its mean. It grows with more points AND with more spread in x. Both make the variance smaller.

And for the intercept:

\[\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_0) = \sigma^2 \left( \cssId{mt-vb0n}{\frac{1}{n}} + \cssId{mt-vb0x}{\frac{\bar{x}^2}{S_{xx}}} \right)\]

  • vb0n: The uncertainty about the height of the line at the centre of the data. It shrinks as n grows.
  • vb0x: The extra uncertainty from carrying the line from the centre of the data back to x = 0. Far from zero, a small error in the slope becomes a big error in the intercept.

The standard error is the standard deviation of the estimator: \(SE(\hat{\beta}_1) = \sqrt{\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1)}\). See the algebra.

One unknown left: σ²

We never see the errors \(u_i\), so we do not know \(\sigma^2\). We estimate it from the residuals:

\[\hat{\sigma}^2 = s^2 = \frac{\cssId{mt-s2num}{\sum \hat{u}_i^2}}{\cssId{mt-s2den}{n-2}}\]

  • s2num: The sum of squared residuals, SSE. The second row of anova(reg) prints it.
  • s2den: Two degrees of freedom are used up estimating the intercept and the slope, so we divide by n − 2, not n.

Dividing by \(n-2\) makes it unbiased: \(E(s^2) = \sigma^2\).

Put it into the variance and take the square root:

\[SE(\hat{\beta}_1) = \frac{\cssId{mt-sehat}{s}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}\]

  • sehat: The residual standard error, the square root of s². summary() calls it “Residual standard error”.

3 · Best among its kind

OLS is unbiased. Is there an unbiased estimator that moves less?

No need to search

There are many ways to draw a line through the data, and many of them are unbiased. They differ in how much they move from one sample to the next.

We do not have to try them one by one. The answer is already known: under four simple assumptions, the unbiased estimator with the smallest variance is OLS, the one we have been using all along.

The assumptions, again

  1. Linear in the parameters, with an additive error: \(y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + u_i\).

  2. The error has mean zero: \(E(u_i) = 0\). On average the line is not too high or too low.

  3. Constant variance: \(\text{Var}(u_i) = \sigma^2\). The scatter around the line is the same width everywhere (homoskedasticity).

  4. Errors are uncorrelated across observations: one day’s error tells you nothing about the next day’s.

Assumptions 1 and 2 give unbiasedness. Adding 3 and 4 gives the next result.

The Gauss–Markov theorem

Under assumptions 1–4, among all linear and unbiased estimators, OLS has the smallest variance:

\[\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1) \leq \text{Var}(\cssId{mt-btilde}{\tilde{\beta}_1}) \qquad \text{and} \qquad \text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_0) \leq \text{Var}(\tilde{\beta}_0)\]

  • btilde: Any other estimator of the slope that is linear in the y values and unbiased. The tilde marks “not OLS”.

where \(\tilde{\beta}_1\) and \(\tilde{\beta}_0\) are any other linear and unbiased estimators.

OLS is BLUE: the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator. What “linear” means · An update from 2022

Another unbiased slope: use only two points

The simplest idea: take the first two observations and draw the line through them. Ignore all the others.

\[\tilde{\beta}_1 = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}\]

Orange: the line through points 1 and 2, slope 4.70. Blue: OLS on all 40 points, slope 2.74.

Is it unbiased? Each point has \(E(y_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i\), so

\[E(\tilde{\beta}_1) = \frac{(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_2) - (\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1)}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{\beta_1 (x_2 - x_1)}{x_2 - x_1} = \beta_1\]

Yes. On average it lands on the true slope, just like OLS.

Which one moves more?

Both estimators are unbiased. Keep the same 40 \(x\) values, draw new errors 5,000 times (true slope 3), and compute both slopes in every sample. Are both centred on 3? Which curve is wider?

Both average 3: OLS 3.01, two points 2.96. But the standard deviation is 0.46 for OLS and 2.86 for the two-point line, about 6 times as large. The two-point line throws away 38 of the 40 points; OLS uses all of them.

Unbiased is not enough. Among linear unbiased estimators, OLS has the smallest variance. The theorem in a picture.

4 · The shape of the slope’s distribution

Centre and spread are settled. To test and to build intervals we also need the shape.

What we have not needed so far

We have not assumed anything about the distribution of the data or of \(\hat{\beta}\).

  • We did not need it to compute \(\hat{\beta}_0\) and \(\hat{\beta}_1\).
  • We did not need it to predict \(\hat{y}_i = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_i\).
  • We did not need it for the mean and the variance of the coefficients.
  • We did not need it for Gauss–Markov.

For inference — confidence intervals and tests — we need the distribution of \(\hat{\beta}\).

The normality assumption

We add one assumption: the errors are normal, \(u_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\).

  • Neither \(y_i\) nor \(x_i\) needs to be normal.
  • But with normal errors, at each value of \(x\) the outcome is normal: \(y_i \mid x_i \sim N(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i,\ \sigma^2)\).
  • For some results this can be relaxed in large samples, thanks to the CLT.

The distributions

A linear combination of normal variables is normal. \(\hat{\beta}_1\) is a weighted sum of the \(y_i\), so:

\[\hat{\beta}_1 \sim \cssId{mt-nb1}{N\left( \beta_1,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2} \right)} \qquad \hat{\beta}_0 \sim N\left( \beta_0,\ \sigma^2 \left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2} \right) \right)\]

  • nb1: A normal distribution centred on the true slope (unbiased), with the variance from section 2. The second number is a variance, as on your formula sheet.

And the variance estimate has its own distribution:

\[\frac{(n-2)\, s^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \cssId{mt-chi}{\chi^2_{n-2}}\]

  • chi: A chi-square with n − 2 degrees of freedom — the same family you used in W06 to test a variance, with n − 2 instead of n − 1.

These are the lines on your formula sheet under Simple Linear Regression.

Big samples: the CLT

With many observations we can drop the normality assumption. By the Central Limit Theorem, approximately:

\[\hat{\beta}_1 \;\cssId{mt-dconv}{\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}}\; N\left( \beta_1,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}} \right) \qquad \hat{\beta}_0 \;\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\; N\left( \beta_0,\ \sigma^2 \left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{S_{xx}} \right) \right)\]

  • dconv: “Converges in distribution”: as n grows, the distribution gets closer and closer to this normal, whatever the shape of the errors.

Your histogram of 1,000 slopes was this bell. Why a sum of errors turns normal.

5 · Testing a slope

Is the effect real, or could it be zero? Is it the number someone promised?

Why test in regression? Advertising

A retailer wants to know if advertising raises sales:

\[\text{Sales}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{AdSpend}_i + u_i\]

Does more advertising actually raise sales? If not, the budget goes elsewhere.

\[H_0: \beta_1 \le 0 \ \text{(ads do not raise sales)} \qquad \text{against} \qquad H_A: \beta_1 > 0 \ \text{(ads raise sales)}\]

Why test in regression? Supply chain

A logistics firm models delivery time on distance to the warehouse:

\[\text{ShipDays}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{Distance}_i + u_i\]

where distance is in kilometres and ShipDays is the average days to delivery.

Its delivery promises assume each extra 100 kilometres adds exactly one day, so one extra kilometre adds 1/100 of a day. Is that right?

\[H_0: \beta_1 = 1/100 \qquad \text{against} \qquad H_A: \beta_1 \neq 1/100\]

If distance matters more, promises are broken. If it matters less, the firm quotes delivery times that are too slow and loses customers.

Why test in regression? A recommendation system

A video app earns money by selling ads. To set its prices, it tells advertisers that a typical user watches 10 hours a week. The app now tests a new recommendation system on some of its users:

\[\text{HoursWatched}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{NewAlgorithm}_i + u_i\]

where HoursWatched is weekly viewing in hours, and NewAlgorithm is 1 if the user saw the new system, 0 otherwise.

Question 1. Is the mean weekly viewing of users without the new system really 10 hours, the number the app gives advertisers? If it is lower, the app overcharges them. If it is higher, the app undercharges them.

\[H_0: \beta_0 = 10 \qquad \text{against} \qquad H_A: \beta_0 \neq 10\]

Question 2. Does mean viewing differ between users with and without the new system? This is a test for a difference in means, done by regression.

\[H_0: \beta_1 = 0 \qquad \text{against} \qquad H_A: \beta_1 \neq 0\]

The test statistic

To test \(H_0: \beta_1 = \beta_1^{0}\) against \(H_A: \beta_1 \neq \beta_1^{0}\), where \(\beta_1^{0}\) is the value under the null:

\[T = \frac{\cssId{mt-tnum}{\hat{\beta}_1 - \beta_1^{0}}}{\cssId{mt-tden}{SE(\hat{\beta}_1)}} = \frac{\hat{\beta}_1 - \beta_1^{0}}{s / \sqrt{S_{xx}}} \sim \cssId{mt-tdist}{t_{n-2}}\]

  • tnum: How far the estimate sits from the value the null hypothesis claims.
  • tden: The standard error: how far a typical estimate falls from the truth. T counts the distance in standard errors.
  • tdist: Student’s t with n − 2 degrees of freedom. The t, not the normal, because s replaces the unknown σ.

For the intercept, \(H_0: \beta_0 = \beta_0^{0}\) against \(H_A: \beta_0 \neq \beta_0^{0}\):

\[T = \frac{\hat{\beta}_0 - \beta_0^{0}}{SE(\hat{\beta}_0)} = \frac{\hat{\beta}_0 - \beta_0^{0}}{\cssId{mt-seb0}{s \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{S_{xx}}}}} \sim t_{n-2}\]

  • seb0: The standard error of the intercept: the square root of its variance from section 2, with s in place of σ. In R: vcov(reg)[1, 1]^0.5.

From here it is the testing procedure from W06: a critical value from qt(), or a p-value from pt().

The p-value

The p-value is the probability of a test statistic at least as extreme as ours, if the null hypothesis were true. Regression tests are usually two-sided:

\[p\text{-value} = 2 \cdot P\left( \cssId{mt-pt}{t_{n-2} > |T|} \right)\]

  • pt: The chance that a t variable lands beyond our statistic on one side. Doubled, because a two-sided test counts both tails. In R: 2 * (1 - pt(T, df)) for a positive T.

The shaded area is the p-value. A small p-value says: if the null were true, a result like ours would be rare.

Is the temperature slope real?

Back to the finance director’s second question. Could the true effect of temperature on trips be zero?

\[H_0: \beta_1 = 0 \qquad \text{against} \qquad H_A: \beta_1 \neq 0\]

If we cannot reject \(H_0\), the client has no evidence that warmer days bring more trips, and no reason to size the fleet by temperature at all.

Test \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) against \(H_A: \beta_1 \neq 0\) at the 5% level, for the regression of bikes$trips on bikes$temp (\(n = 781\)).

t_stat

6 · A range for the slope

A test says yes or no. An interval says how big.

The confidence interval for a coefficient

\(T\) has a \(t_{n-2}\) distribution, so

\[P\left( -t_{n-2,\,1-\alpha/2} < \cssId{mt-pivot}{\frac{\hat{\beta}_1 - \beta_1}{SE(\hat{\beta}_1)}} < t_{n-2,\,1-\alpha/2} \right) = \cssId{mt-conf}{1 - \alpha}\]

  • pivot: The test statistic with the true slope in it. Whatever β1 is, this ratio follows a t with n − 2 degrees of freedom.
  • conf: The confidence level: 0.95 when α = 0.05.

Rearranged for \(\beta_1\):

\[CI_{\beta_1} = \cssId{mt-cib}{\hat{\beta}_1} \pm \cssId{mt-ciq}{t_{n-2,\,1-\alpha/2}} \cdot \cssId{mt-cise}{\frac{s}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}}\]

  • cib: The estimate, the centre of the interval.
  • ciq: The critical value, qt(1 - alpha/2, df = n - 2). About 1.96 for 95% when n is large.
  • cise: The standard error of the slope, SE(β̂1) — vcov(reg)[2, 2]^0.5 in R.

And for the intercept:

\[CI_{\beta_0} = \hat{\beta}_0 \pm t_{n-2,\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot \cssId{mt-ciseb0}{s \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{S_{xx}}}}\]

  • ciseb0: The standard error of the intercept, SE(β̂0) — vcov(reg)[1, 1]^0.5 in R.

7 · Predicting the average day

From the slope to a forecast. First for the average of many days.

Two different questions about 22 °C

Your client runs Ecobici. Tomorrow will be 22 °C, and so will many days this season. She has two decisions to make:

  1. The season budget. She pays the rebalancing crews for the whole season, so she needs the average number of trips on a 22 °C day.
  2. Tonight. She has to decide how many bikes to put in the docks for tomorrow, so she needs the number of trips on that one day.

Which of the two numbers can you give more precisely: the average of many 22 °C days, or the trips on one 22 °C day? Look at the days in the data that were close to 22 °C.

What the 22 °C days show

One day near 22 °C had 10,941 trips; another had 37,721. Tomorrow could land anywhere in that range: the second question is hard.

Their average is pinned down far better: its standard error is \(7{,}459 / \sqrt{19} = 1{,}711\) trips. The regression uses all 781 days, so its average at 22 °C is tighter still. Same point forecast, \(\hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 \times 22\); different uncertainty. Compute these numbers yourself

Why the average is easier

Every day is the line plus that day’s own error:

\[\text{trips}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{temp}_i + \cssId{mt-udaily}{u_i}\]

  • udaily: Everything else that moves trips on day i. It is positive on some days and negative on others, and zero on average.
  • \(u_i\) is everything else that moves trips that day: a match at the stadium, a metro strike, an afternoon of rain.
  • Over many days at 22 °C these errors go up and down and cancel out, since \(E(u_i) = 0\). The average of many 22 °C days is just the line at 22 °C: \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 \times 22\).
  • So for the season budget, the only uncertainty left is how well we estimated the \(\beta\)s.

One day keeps its error. That is section 8: mean response or new response?

The mean response

The average number of trips at \(x = x_0\) is estimated by

\[\hat{y}(x_0) = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 \cssId{mt-x0}{x_0}\]

  • x0: The value of x we predict at — here 22 °C. It is chosen by us, not drawn from the data.

Its expectation is the true average:

\[E\left( \hat{y}(x_0) \right) = \cssId{mt-truemean}{\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_0}\]

  • truemean: The population average of y at x0 — the point on the true line. Unbiased coefficients give an unbiased forecast of it.

Its variance:

\[\text{Var}\left( \hat{y}(x_0) \right) = \sigma^2 \left( \cssId{mt-mrn}{\frac{1}{n}} + \cssId{mt-mrx}{\frac{(x_0 - \bar{x})^2}{S_{xx}}} \right)\]

  • mrn: Uncertainty about the height of the line at the centre of the data. Shrinks as n grows.
  • mrx: Uncertainty about the tilt of the line. It grows with the distance between x0 and the mean of x: a small error in the slope matters more the further you go.

Why the daily error \(u\) is not in this variance, and why it stays for one day: see the derivation.

With normal errors: \(\hat{y}(x_0) \sim N\left( \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_0,\ \sigma^2 \left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_0 - \bar{x})^2}{S_{xx}} \right) \right)\) — the Average (mean) prediction line on your formula sheet.

The confidence interval for the mean response

As before: estimate \(\pm\) critical value \(\times\) standard error.

\[CI_{\hat{y}(x_0)} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_0 \pm t_{n-2,\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot \cssId{mt-semr}{s \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_0 - \bar{x})^2}{S_{xx}}}}\]

  • semr: The standard error of the mean response. Replace σ with s and take the square root of the variance on the previous slide.

8 · Predicting one day

The average of many days is not tomorrow. One day carries its own error.

Mean response or new response?

Both of your client’s questions start from the same number, \(\hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 \times 22\).

  • Mean response, the season budget: many 22 °C days. Their errors cancel out, so only the error in the \(\beta\)s is left. Back to why.
  • New response, tonight’s docks: one day, tomorrow. Its error \(u_0\) has nothing to cancel against.

The new response

New response. The number of trips tomorrow, one day at 22 °C. This decides how many bikes go in the docks tonight.

  • With one day, its error \(u_0\) does not cancel. Tomorrow may bring the match, the strike or the rain.
  • In the mean response those surprises average out. Here they stay.
  • So there is more uncertainty, and a wider interval: \(\text{Var}(\text{new response}) = \text{Var}(\text{mean response}) + \text{Var}(u_0)\).
  • This interval needs the errors to be normal. The CLT is not enough, because the interval is about one error \(u_0\), not about an average.

The prediction interval

For a new observation at \(x_0\), the error we make is \(y_0 - \hat{y}(x_0)\):

\[\text{Var}\left( y_0 - \hat{y}(x_0) \right) = \text{Var}\left( \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_0 \right) + \text{Var}(u_0) = \sigma^2 \left( \cssId{mt-pi1}{1} + \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_0 - \bar{x})^2}{S_{xx}} \right)\]

  • pi1: The new day’s own error, u0, with variance σ². It does not shrink as n grows: more data pins down the line, not tomorrow’s surprise.

So, the New observation line on your formula sheet: \(y(x_0) \sim N\left( \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_0,\ \sigma^2 \left( 1 + \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_0 - \bar{x})^2}{S_{xx}} \right) \right)\)

And the interval:

\[PI_{y(x_0)} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_0 \pm t_{n-2,\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot \cssId{mt-sepi}{s \sqrt{1 + \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_0 - \bar{x})^2}{S_{xx}}}}\]

  • sepi: The standard error for one new observation. Compared with the mean response, only the 1 is new — and it is usually the biggest term.

Summary: simple linear regression

Concept Key formula or idea
Model \(y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + u_i\)
OLS Minimises \(SSE = \sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2\)
Slope \(\hat{\beta}_1 = \text{Cov}(x, y) / \text{Var}(x)\)
\(R^2\) Share of the variation in \(y\) explained by \(x\)
Standard error \(SE(\hat{\beta}_1) = s / \sqrt{S_{xx}}\), with \(s^2 = SSE/(n-2)\)
Test \(T = (\hat{\beta}_1 - \beta_1^{0}) / SE(\hat{\beta}_1) \sim t_{n-2}\)
CI for \(\beta_1\) \(\hat{\beta}_1 \pm t_{n-2,\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot SE(\hat{\beta}_1)\)
CI for the mean at \(x_0\) \(\hat{y}(x_0) \pm t_{n-2,\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot s\sqrt{1/n + (x_0 - \bar{x})^2/S_{xx}}\)
PI for one new \(y\) at \(x_0\) \(\hat{y}(x_0) \pm t_{n-2,\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot s\sqrt{1 + 1/n + (x_0 - \bar{x})^2/S_{xx}}\)

Key takeaways

  • OLS finds the line that minimises the squared residuals.
  • \(\hat{\beta}_1\) is the change in \(y\) for a one-unit change in \(x\), and it moves from sample to sample.
  • Its standard error shrinks with more data, less noise and more spread in \(x\).
  • We test and build intervals for coefficients with the \(t\) distribution, \(n - 2\) degrees of freedom.
  • Intervals for the mean response are narrower than for a new response, which carries its own error.

What you should be able to do

  • Explain why \(\hat{\beta}_1\) is a random variable, and what makes its standard error small.
  • Build \(s\), \(S_{xx}\) and \(SE(\hat{\beta}_1)\) by hand, and check with vcov().
  • State both hypotheses for any test on \(\beta_0\) or \(\beta_1\), two-sided or one-sided, with a null value of 0 or any other number.
  • Compute \(T\), the critical value with qt() and the p-value with pt(); find the same numbers in summary().
  • Build a confidence interval for a coefficient, and read a test off it.
  • Build the confidence interval for a mean response and the prediction interval for one new observation, by hand and with predict(), and say why one is wider.
  • Say when a prediction is an extrapolation.

📝 Exam practice 3 — Scatter and uncertainty

[P2 #8]

Is I or II higher, or are they equal? Explain. For a regression line, the uncertainty in the slope estimate \(\hat{\beta}_1\) is higher when:

    1. there is a lot of scatter around the regression line, or
    1. there is very little scatter around the regression line.
  1. is higher. More scatter means a larger residual standard error \(s\), and

\[SE(\hat{\beta}_1) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}}\]

so the standard error of the slope grows with it: ten times the scatter gives ten times the standard error.

When points sit tightly around the line, only a narrow range of slopes fits them. When they are spread out, many different slopes fit about equally well.

Appendix — what “linear” means here

A linear estimator is a weighted sum of the \(y_i\):

\[\tilde{\beta}_1 = \sum_i \cssId{mt-ci}{c_i}\, y_i\]

  • ci: A weight for each observation. It may depend on the x values, but never on the y values.

OLS is one of them. Since \(\sum (x_i - \bar{x})\bar{y} = 0\):

\[\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})\, y_i}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2} \qquad \text{so} \qquad \cssId{mt-cols}{c_i^{OLS} = \frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{S_{xx}}}\]

  • cols: The OLS weight on observation i. Positive to the right of the mean of x, negative to the left, and largest in size at the extremes.

Points far from \(\bar{x}\) get the biggest weights. That is the plank from the slider slide again.

Appendix — the variance of the slope and the intercept

Treat the \(x_i\) as fixed. Then \(\text{Var}(y_i) = \text{Var}(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + u_i) = \text{Var}(u_i) = \sigma^2\), and

\[\begin{aligned} \text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1) &= \text{Var}\left( \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2} \right) = \text{Var}\left( \sum_i \frac{(x_i - \bar{x})\, y_i}{S_{xx}} \right) \\ &= \sum_i \left( \frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{S_{xx}} \right)^2 \text{Var}(y_i) = \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}} \end{aligned}\]

The third step uses \(\text{Cov}(u_i, u_j) = 0\): the variance of a sum of uncorrelated terms is the sum of their variances.

For the intercept:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_0) &= \text{Var}(\bar{y} - \hat{\beta}_1 \bar{x}) = \text{Var}(\bar{y}) + \bar{x}^2\, \text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1) - 2 \bar{x}\, \underbrace{\text{Cov}(\bar{y}, \hat{\beta}_1)}_{0} \\ &= \frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \bar{x}^2 \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}} = \sigma^2 \left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{\bar{x}^2}{S_{xx}} \right) \end{aligned}\]

Appendix — why the slope is normal in big samples

The OLS slope is a linear combination of the errors:

\[\hat{\beta}_1 = \beta_1 + \sum_i \underbrace{\frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{S_{xx}}}_{\text{weight}} u_i\]

Call each weighted error \(Z_i = \frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{S_{xx}} u_i\), so that \(\hat{\beta}_1 - \beta_1 = \sum_i Z_i\).

By the Central Limit Theorem, a sum of many independent, mean-zero terms is approximately normal:

\[\sum_i Z_i \;\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\; N\left( 0,\ \text{Var}\left( \sum_i Z_i \right) \right)\]

And

\[\text{Var}\left( \sum_i Z_i \right) = \sum_i \text{Var}(Z_i) = \frac{\sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{S_{xx}^2}\, \sigma^2 = \frac{S_{xx}}{S_{xx}^2}\, \sigma^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}}\]

so

\[\hat{\beta}_1 - \beta_1 \;\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\; N\left( 0,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{S_{xx}} \right)\]

Appendix — why the error cancels in the average but not on one day

On any day at temperature \(x_0\), trips are \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_0 + u\), with \(E(u) = 0\) and \(\text{Var}(u) = \sigma^2\), independently from day to day.

The average of \(m\) days at \(x_0\):

\[\bar{y}_m = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^{m} \left( \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_0 + u_j \right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_0 + \cssId{mt-ubar}{\bar{u}_m}\]

  • ubar: The average of the m daily errors. Good and bad days pull it in opposite directions.

\[E(\bar{u}_m) = 0, \qquad \text{Var}(\bar{u}_m) = \frac{\sigma^2}{m} \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \quad \text{as } m \to \infty\]

So the average of many 22 °C days is \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_0\) itself, with no error left. The interval only has to cover the error in our estimate of the line: \(\text{Var}\left( \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_0 \right) = \sigma^2 \left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_0 - \bar{x})^2}{S_{xx}} \right)\).

Appendix — one day keeps its error

Tomorrow is one day: \(y_0 = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_0 + u_0\). Our forecast is \(\hat{y}(x_0) = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_0\), so the forecast error is

\[y_0 - \hat{y}(x_0) = \left( \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_0 - \hat{\beta}_0 - \hat{\beta}_1 x_0 \right) + \cssId{mt-u0new}{u_0}\]

  • u0new: Tomorrow’s own error. There is only one of it, so nothing averages it away.

\(u_0\) belongs to tomorrow, so it is independent of the 781 days used to fit the line. The two variances add:

\[\text{Var}\left( y_0 - \hat{y}(x_0) \right) = \sigma^2 \left( \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_0 - \bar{x})^2}{S_{xx}} \right) + \sigma^2 = \sigma^2 \left( 1 + \frac{1}{n} + \frac{(x_0 - \bar{x})^2}{S_{xx}} \right)\]

The extra \(\sigma^2\) does not shrink with more data. A bigger sample pins down the line, not tomorrow’s surprise.
