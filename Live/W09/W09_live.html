1 · How far off is the line?

Last week the line gave an answer. This week: how good is that answer, and can we trust it?

The question on the table

Back to Ecobici. In W07 you fitted

\[\widehat{\text{trips}} = 16{,}893 + 723.6 \times \text{temp}\]

on 781 weekdays, and found \(R^2 = 0.088\).

The client’s finance director reads the report and says:

Temperature explains 8.8% of ridership. So the model is useless. Throw it away.

Two separate questions hide in that sentence:

  1. How well does the line fit — and is the fit real, or luck?
  2. Are the assumptions behind the line true? If not, which numbers in the report are wrong?

The first question is model fit. The second is diagnostics. This week is both.

The typical miss

\(R^2\) is a share. The client plans her fleet in trips, so she also needs to know how far off a forecast typically is — in trips.

The residual variance is the average squared miss:

\[s^2 = \frac{\cssId{mt-sumu2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \hat{u}_i^2}}{\cssId{mt-nminus2}{n-2}}\]

  • sumu2: The sum of squared residuals — the same SSE you met in W07. Every day’s miss, squared, then added up.
  • nminus2: We divide by n − 2, not n. Two numbers, the intercept and the slope, were estimated from the same data, and each one uses up one piece of information.

Its square root, \(s = \sqrt{s^2}\), is the residual standard error. It is measured in the units of \(y\) — here, trips.

2 · Is the fit real?

A small \(R^2\) could be a weak relationship — or no relationship at all, plus luck. The F-test tells them apart.

A question from a restaurant chain

A restaurant chain orders ingredients from a supplier every day. Some deliveries arrive with ingredients missing.

  • \(x\) = the hour the order is placed (1 to 24)
  • \(y\) = the number of missing ingredients in the delivery

Should the chain move its orders to a different hour? Only if the hour really explains part of the mistakes.

That is what an analysis of variance (ANOVA) checks. It compares two things:

  • the variation in \(y\) that the regression explains — the signal;
  • the variation it leaves unexplained — the noise.

ANOVA as a test

The null hypothesis says the regression explains no more than a model with no predictor at all:

\[H_0: \beta_1 = 0 \qquad \text{against} \qquad H_A: \beta_1 \neq 0\]

  • \(H_0\): the hour does not matter. The best forecast is just the average number of missing ingredients.
  • \(H_A\): the hour matters. The regression explains some of the variation.

If we reject \(H_0\), the regression adds value.

One day, three distances

\[\underbrace{y_i - \bar{y}}_{\text{total}} = \underbrace{(\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})}_{\cssId{mt-explained}{\text{explained}}} + \underbrace{(y_i - \hat{y}_i)}_{\cssId{mt-unexplained}{\text{unexplained}}}\]

  • explained: How far the line moves this day away from the average day. Temperature gets the credit for this part.
  • unexplained: The residual — how far the day still sits from the line.

From one day to all days

Square each distance and add over all days:

\[\cssId{mt-sst2}{\sum_{i}(y_i-\bar{y})^2} = \cssId{mt-ssr2}{\sum_{i}(\hat{y}_i-\bar{y})^2} + \cssId{mt-sse2}{\sum_{i}(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2}\]

  • sst2: SST, the total sum of squares. It has n − 1 degrees of freedom: the mean of y was estimated first.
  • ssr2: SSR, the regression sum of squares. One degree of freedom — the slope. It also equals the squared slope times the sum of squared deviations of x.
  • sse2: SSE, the error sum of squares. It has n − 2 degrees of freedom: the intercept and the slope were both estimated.
Piece Formula Degrees of freedom
\(SST\) \(\sum_i(y_i-\bar{y})^2\) \(n-1\)
\(SSR\) \(\sum_i(\hat{y}_i-\bar{y})^2 = \hat{\beta}_1^2 \sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2\) \(1\)
\(SSE\) \(\sum_i(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2\) \(n-2\)

The degrees of freedom add up too: \((n-1) = 1 + (n-2)\).

Good fit, bad fit

  • Left: most of the distance from the mean is green. The line explains a lot.
  • Right: most of it is red. The line explains little.

The F statistic

Divide each sum of squares by its degrees of freedom. These are the mean squares:

\[\cssId{mt-msr}{MSR} = \frac{SSR}{1} \qquad \cssId{mt-mse}{MSE} = \frac{SSE}{n-2} = s^2\]

  • msr: Mean square for the regression: explained variation per degree of freedom used.
  • mse: Mean square error: unexplained variation per degree of freedom left. It is exactly the s² from section 1.

The test statistic compares them:

\[F = \frac{\cssId{mt-fnum}{SSR / df_R}}{\cssId{mt-fden}{SSE / df_E}} = \frac{MSR}{MSE}\]

  • fnum: Signal: explained variation per degree of freedom. df_R = 1 in simple regression.
  • fden: Noise: unexplained variation per degree of freedom. df_E = n − 2.

If \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) is true, \(F\) follows an \(F\) distribution with \(1\) and \(n-2\) degrees of freedom. A large \(F\) is evidence for \(H_A: \beta_1 \neq 0\).

Reject \(H_0\) at level \(\alpha\) when \(F > F_{1-\alpha;\,1,\,n-2}\). The p-value is \(P(F_{1,\,n-2} \geq F)\). Why the ratio works.

What to try on it

  • Set the true slope to 0. Press New sample a few times. \(F\) lands in the unshaded part about 95% of the time.
  • Keep the slope small, say 0.3, and raise \(n\). The same weak slope becomes clearly significant.
  • Raise the noise. \(SSE\) grows, \(F\) falls, and the verdict can flip.

A weak relationship measured on many observations can give a large \(F\). That is the Ecobici situation.

The same test, seen as two models

The \(F\) statistic can be written with \(SST\) and \(SSE\) only:

\[F = \frac{SSR/df_R}{SSE/df_E} = \frac{\cssId{mt-drop}{(SST - SSE)/(df_T - df_E)}}{SSE/df_E}\]

  • drop: How much the unexplained variation falls when you add x, per degree of freedom spent on it.
  • Full model, with \(x\): \(\hat{y}_i = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x_i\). What it leaves unexplained is \(SSE\).
  • Restricted model, intercept only: \(\hat{y}_i = \bar{y}\). What it leaves unexplained is \(SST\).

The top of \(F\) measures how much the unexplained part shrinks when we move from the restricted model to the full one. If it shrinks a lot, reject the restricted model.

With one regressor this is exactly the ANOVA test. From W10 on, with several regressors, the same idea tests whether a group of extra predictors helps.

Three tests, one answer

With a single regressor, three tests ask the same question:

Test Null against alternative Statistic
correlation test \(H_0: \rho = 0\) against \(H_A: \rho \neq 0\) \(T = \dfrac{r\sqrt{n-2}}{\sqrt{1-r^2}} \sim t_{n-2}\)
t-test on the slope (W08) \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) against \(H_A: \beta_1 \neq 0\) \(T = \dfrac{\hat{\beta}_1}{SE(\hat{\beta}_1)} \sim t_{n-2}\)
F-test \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) against \(H_A: \beta_1 \neq 0\) \(F = \dfrac{MSR}{MSE} \sim F_{1,\,n-2}\)

The correlation test is on your formula sheet. Check the claim on the Ecobici data.

3 · What the residuals should look like

The errors are never seen. The residuals are our only view of them.

The assumptions, again

  1. Linear in the parameters, with an additive error: \(y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + u_i\).
  2. The error has mean zero: \(E(u_i) = 0\).
  3. Constant variance: \(\text{Var}(u_i) = \sigma^2\).
  4. Errors uncorrelated across observations: \(\text{Cov}(u_i, u_j) = 0\).

One more is needed for t-tests, F-tests and intervals in small samples:

  1. The error is normal: \(u_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\).

Which tool checks which

Assumption Look at A healthy picture
linearity residuals against fitted values no curve, no arch
constant variance residuals against fitted values a band of even width
uncorrelated errors residuals in time order; \(\hat{u}_i\) against \(\hat{u}_{i-1}\) no runs, no tilt
normality histogram, Q-Q plot, Jarque–Bera test bell shape, straight line, small JB

The first three are checked with pictures. Normality has a picture and a formal test.

The ad budget question

An online furniture retailer ran one month of digital ads in 120 Mexican cities. Each city got a different budget.

The data frame campaigns:

Marketing wants one number: how many pesos of sales does one more peso of ads bring? If that number is the same at every budget, the answer is simple — raise every budget.

The model is still linear in \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) — assumption 1 only asks for that. What changed is the regressor.

5 · Is the spread constant?

Assumption 3: the same variance everywhere. When it fails, the residuals fan out.

Constant variance: a heteroskedastic example

Plot the residuals against \(\hat{y}\) and ask: does the vertical spread change as \(\hat{y}\) changes?

The spread shrinks as \(\hat{y}\) grows. The variance of the error is not constant. This is called heteroskedasticity.

Constant variance: a homoskedastic example

Same line, same \(x\). Now the band has the same width everywhere: homoskedasticity.

What goes wrong here? — another residual plot

Constant variance fails. The spread is wide at both ends and narrow in the middle — a bow tie. The line itself is fine; the scatter around it is not the same everywhere.

The fan

  • Poor households all spend about the same on food. Rich households differ a lot.
  • One band of \(\pm s = \pm 752\) pesos is too wide for the poor and too narrow for the rich.

What a fan breaks — and what it does not

  • The slope is still unbiased. Unbiasedness needs only \(E(u_i) = 0\), not constant variance.
  • The standard-error formula of W08 assumes one common \(\sigma^2\). With a fan, the reported standard errors are wrong — usually too small.
  • So the t-statistics, p-values and confidence intervals are wrong too.
  • Prediction intervals have one width for everybody: too wide for the poor, too narrow for the rich.

A manager who reads only the slope sees nothing wrong. The damage is in the precision attached to it.

The Gauss–Markov theorem

Under assumptions 1–4, among all linear and unbiased estimators, OLS has the smallest variance:

\[\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1) \leq \text{Var}(\cssId{mt-b1prime}{\hat{\beta}_1'}) \qquad \text{and} \qquad \text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_0) \leq \text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_0')\]

  • b1prime: Any other estimator of the slope that is linear in the y values and unbiased.

OLS is BLUE: the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator.

A linear estimator is a weighted sum of the \(y_i\): \(\hat{\beta}_1' = \sum_i c_i y_i\). For OLS the weights are

\[\hat{\beta}_1 = \frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})y_i}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})^2} \qquad \text{so} \qquad c_i^{OLS} = \frac{\cssId{mt-cw}{x_i-\bar{x}}}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar{x})^2}\]

  • cw: Days far from the average temperature get more weight in the slope. Days right at the average get almost none.

Research continues: Hansen (2022) showed the word “linear” can be dropped. Under the same assumptions OLS is the best of all unbiased estimators (BUE).

Exam question — Heteroskedastic errors

Which of the statements A and B are true?

In 1,000 samples where the error spread grows with \(x\), the slopes average 2.000, the true value of 2. So A is false: OLS stays unbiased when the variance is not constant.

B is true. Gauss–Markov needs constant variance (assumption 3) to make OLS the minimum-variance estimator. With \(\text{Var}(\epsilon_i) = \sigma_i^2\), an estimator that gives less weight to the noisy observations can beat OLS.

Errors that remember

When the data come in time order, check whether each residual looks like the one before:

  1. Plot each residual \(\hat{u}_i\) against the previous one, \(\hat{u}_{i-1}\): \(\hat{u}_2\) against \(\hat{u}_1\), \(\hat{u}_5\) against \(\hat{u}_4\), and so on.

  3. Do the same with longer gaps: \(\hat{u}_i\) against \(\hat{u}_{i-2}\), \(\hat{u}_{i-3}\), … Note the correlation at each lag: \(\rho(\hat{u}_i, \hat{u}_{i-1})\), \(\rho(\hat{u}_i, \hat{u}_{i-2})\), \(\rho(\hat{u}_i, \hat{u}_{i-3})\).

If the errors are uncorrelated, every one of these correlations should be close to 0.

From left to right the tilt fades. At the right, knowing yesterday’s residual tells you nothing about today’s.

7 · Are the errors normal?

Assumption 5. Pictures first, then a formal test.

Why normality matters

  • The slope, the intercept and \(R^2\) do not need normal errors. OLS computes them either way.
  • The t-test, the F-test and the intervals of W08 use the \(t\) and \(F\) distributions. Those are exact only when the errors are normal.
  • With a large sample, the central limit theorem makes \(\hat{\beta}_1\) close to normal anyway, so the tests still work approximately. With a small sample there is no such protection.

Histograms: some departures are obvious

Top: a bell. Bottom: a long right tail — clearly not normal.

Histograms: some are not obvious

This looks almost like a bell. But the errors come from a \(t_3\) distribution: too many values far from the centre. A histogram cannot show this well. We need a sharper tool.

The Q-Q plot

A quantile–quantile plot compares the quantiles of the residuals with the quantiles they would have if they came from a normal distribution.

  1. Standardise the residuals: subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation.

  2. Sort them from smallest to largest. Give each an index \(i\): 1 is the smallest, \(n\) the largest.

  3. Each one’s cumulative probability is the share of the data below it:

    \[CP_i = \frac{\cssId{mt-qqi}{i}}{\cssId{mt-qqn1}{n+1}}\]

  4. Find the standard normal quantile with that probability, qnorm(CP).

  5. Plot the sample values against the normal quantiles. If the residuals are normal, the points lie on the 45° line.

  • qqi: The position of the residual after sorting. i = 1 is the smallest.
  • qqn1: Dividing by n + 1, not n, keeps the largest residual below probability 1 — the normal quantile of 1 would be infinite.

Jarque–Bera: skewness

A normal distribution is symmetric: its skewness is 0.

\[S = \frac{\cssId{mt-m3}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^3}}{\left(\cssId{mt-m2}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2}\right)^{3/2}}\]

  • m3: The average cubed deviation. Cubing keeps the sign, so a long right tail makes it positive and a long left tail makes it negative.
  • m2: The average squared deviation — the variance with n in the denominator. Raising it to 3/2 removes the units.

Jarque–Bera: kurtosis

The kurtosis of a normal distribution is 3. Excess kurtosis subtracts that 3, so for a normal distribution it is 0:

\[EK = \frac{\cssId{mt-m4}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^4}}{\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2\right)^{2}} \cssId{mt-minus3}{- 3}\]

  • m4: The average fourth-power deviation. Values far from the centre dominate it, so it measures how heavy the tails are.
  • minus3: A normal distribution scores exactly 3, so after subtracting 3 a normal scores 0.

All three have variance 1. The dashed orange curve is the normal, for reference.

The Jarque–Bera statistic

\[JB = \frac{\cssId{mt-jbn}{n}}{6}\left(\cssId{mt-jbs}{S^2} + \frac{\cssId{mt-jbk}{EK^2}}{4}\right)\]

  • jbn: The sample size. The same skewness is stronger evidence in a big sample than in a small one.
  • jbs: Squared, so skewness in either direction counts against normality.
  • jbk: Squared, so tails that are too heavy or too light both count.
  • \(JB\) is large when the skewness is far from 0 or the excess kurtosis is far from 0.
  • If the data come from a normal distribution, \(JB\) follows a chi-square distribution with 2 degrees of freedom — one for skewness, one for kurtosis.

\(H_0\): the residuals come from a normal distribution (JB is small) against \(H_A\): they do not (JB is large).

Only a large \(JB\) is evidence against \(H_0\), so the test is one-sided. Reject at level \(\alpha\) if \(JB > \chi^2_{1-\alpha;\,2}\). The p-value is \(P(\chi^2_2 \geq JB)\).

8 · One point can move the line

Not an assumption — a warning. Some observations have far more say in the slope than others.

🎛️ Drag one point

slope without the red point ?    slope with it ?

Leverage

  • A point in the middle of the \(x\) range, moved up or down, mostly shifts the line up or down. The slope barely changes.
  • A point far from \(\bar{x}\) acts like a long lever. Moved up or down, it rotates the line.
  • A point far from the others in \(x\) has high leverage. If it also sits off the line, it is influential: one observation decides the slope.

An influential point is not an assumption failure. It is a warning that the estimate is fragile. The check: fit the model with and without it, and compare.

Cold days, empty streets

In W07 the Ecobici residual histogram had a long left tail. Section 7 found the same tail in the Q-Q plot. Those days — Christmas, New Year’s Day, Holy Week — are also some of the coldest days in the data.

The Ecobici verdict

Fit: \(R^2 = 0.088\) and a typical miss of 5,302 trips. But \(F = 75.3\): the temperature effect is real.

Diagnostics: the line is the right shape. But the residuals are strongly autocorrelated (0.55), not normal (JB = 1,390), and their spread is larger on cold days, which is where the holidays fall.

Is the slope itself right?

A firm regresses employees’ salary on their years of education to set its pay scale.

  • What kinds of factors end up in the error \(u_i\)?
  • Are they correlated with education?
  • Would the slope estimator be unbiased?

Ability, family background, connections and ambition all sit in \(u_i\), and all of them move with education. Then \(E(u_i) = 0\) fails at a given level of education: the error is higher, on average, for the more educated. The slope picks up part of their effect and is biased — usually upwards.

No residual plot can show this. OLS makes the residuals uncorrelated with \(x\) by construction, even when the true errors are not.

🎛️ When the error moves with x

The dashed green line is the truth: \(y = 0.5x\). Grey points are the data, the orange line is the OLS fit.

true line: intercept 0.00, slope 0.50   |   OLS: intercept —, slope —
  • Both sliders at 0: the assumptions hold, and OLS lands on the truth, up to noise.
  • Move the mean of the error: the whole cloud shifts, and the intercept absorbs it.
  • Make the error move with \(x\): the slope is biased, and more data does not fix it.

Appendix — optional

The algebra behind the F-test.

Appendix — why the F-test works

Whether \(\beta_1 = 0\) or not, the error mean square estimates \(\sigma^2\) without bias:

\[E\left(\frac{SSE}{df_E}\right) = E\left(\frac{\sum_i \hat{u}_i^2}{n-2}\right) = \sigma^2, \qquad \frac{SSE}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-2}\]

Only if the null is true (\(\beta_1 = 0\)):

\[E\left(\frac{SSR}{df_R}\right) = E\left(\frac{\sum_i(\hat{y}_i-\bar{y})^2}{1}\right) = \sigma^2, \qquad \frac{SSR}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{1}\]

So under the null the ratio of the two, each divided by its degrees of freedom, is

\[F = \frac{SSR/df_R}{SSE/df_E} \sim F_{1,\,n-2}\]

If the alternative is true,

\[E(SSR) = \sigma^2 + \beta_1^2 \sum_i (x_i - \bar{x})^2\]

so the numerator tends to be larger than the denominator and \(F\) is large. When the model explains \(y\) well, the explained part outweighs the unexplained part.
