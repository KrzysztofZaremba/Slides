A decision you’ll be able to make

You manage inventory for Walmart México. It’s April. How many umbrellas should each store stock for June?

  • Order too few → empty shelves, lost sales, unhappy customers.
  • Order too many → cash tied up in stock that sits until next year.

The right number is a forecast: last year’s June sales, the trend, the rainy-season pattern, a promotion you’re planning — turned into a decision, with a sense of how uncertain it is.

By the end of this course, you’ll build exactly this kind of model — on your own data.

Put that line in your script and your analysis reruns from scratch — clicking through the menus every time does not.

Introduction to Forecasting

Tools will often depend on the horizon and data availability

Forecasting Horizons

  • Very Short Horizon:
    • High-Frequency Trading: Real-time price predictions for financial trading
  • Short Horizon:
    • Public bikes: Predict the availability of bikes at bike station and adjust the number
  • Long Horizon:
    • New obesity drug: Forecasting number of potential patients and their resources

Overview of Forecasting Techniques

1. Qualitative Forecasting

  • Based on subjective judgment and expert opinions
  • Suitable for unique situations or new markets
  • Examples: economic impact of an oil-price change, political stability in a region

2. Quantitative Forecasting

  • Uses historical data and numerical techniques
  • Suitable when data is available and past patterns carry into the future
  • Examples: demand for a product you already sell, next quarter’s revenue

We take them in that order: qualitative first, then quantitative — which is where the rest of the course lives.

Methods of Qualitative Forecasting

The oracle of Delphi

Delphi Method

A structured process to reach consensus on complex, uncertain, long-term questions:

  1. Select a group of experts.
  2. Keep them anonymous — they don’t talk to each other.
  3. Ask them to answer a questionnaire.
  4. Collect the initial responses.
  5. Compile them into a summary.
  6. Send the summary back; collect refined answers.
  7. Repeat until consensus (or no further improvement).

Delphi Method — example

Determining AI threats.

  • Question: what are the biggest risks from AI development?
  • Panel: computer scientists, engineers, AI-company CEOs, ethicists.
  • Send questionnaires on potential threats → compile → send back.
  • Several rounds until the panel converges.
  • Output: a ranked list of the most probable risks.

Brainstorming

A creative technique for generating ideas — free thinking, building on others.

  • Form a group (no need for experts).
  • State the problem clearly.
  • Encourage ideas, no matter how crazy.
  • Combine and build on each other’s ideas.
  • Document, then synthesize.

Example — employee engagement: a tech firm’s HR gathers reps from HR, IT, and other teams to generate ideas for an app that boosts engagement, then implements the most promising ones.

Panel of Experts

Assemble knowledgeable people in the same place, at the same time.

  • They meet, share insights, and discuss openly.
  • Helps reach well-informed decisions.
  • Often ends in a report with conclusions.

Example — environmental policy: a government agency convenes scientists, economists, conservationists, and policymakers to debate options and draft comprehensive environmental policy.

Focus Groups

Gather a diverse set of participants — not necessarily experts.

  • Share perceptions, attitudes, and opinions.
  • Produce qualitative data and consumer insight.

Example — a new TV show: before launch, a network gathers viewers from varied demographics to gauge appeal and fine-tune the show and its marketing.

The four methods — at a glance

One business use for each method:

  • Delphi → ranking AI threats with an anonymous expert panel.
  • Brainstorming → an HR team’s ideas for an employee-engagement app.
  • Panel of Experts → a convened panel drafting environmental policy.
  • Focus Groups → viewers gauging a new TV show before launch.

Methods of Quantitative Forecasting

Quantitative forecasting — when can you trust it?

Quantitative methods use historical data and numerical techniques. They work when the continuity assumption holds:

past trends and relationships continue into the future.

When does it hold?

Interest rates and investment — the mechanism linking them is stable.

When does it break?

Covid cases and deaths, once vaccines arrived — the relationship itself changed, so the past stopped predicting the future.

Time Series Forecasting

  • Time series data: collection of data points for a single unit (one firm, one person, one country) ordered chronologically. Can be one or more variables.
  • Time series forecasting: identifying patterns and trends in historical data to predict future values

Explanatory Models

  • We have data on both the variable of interest and other variables related to it
  • We consider how other variables impact the outcome of interest
  • We use these relationships to make forecasts

Steps of Forecasting

  1. Problem Definition
    • Clearly define the forecasting objective.
  2. Gather Data
    • Identify and collect relevant data.
  3. Preliminary Explanatory Analysis
    • Understand data characteristics and relationships.
  4. Choosing and Fitting the Model
    • Select and fit the appropriate forecasting model.
  5. Evaluating the Model
    • Assess the model’s performance using historical data.
  6. Communicate the results
    • Visualize and interpret the outcomes and write a report or presentation
